# Chapter Eleven: Testimony

"An umbrella," said the old woman. "Do they mean I was useful when it rained, and then got left in the hall?"

She was small and broad and somewhere past eighty, with hands that had done a great deal of washing up, and she had not sat down yet. Mira had a feeling she was not a woman who sat down much.

"They mean you kept people dry," said Mira. "You held the coats at funerals. You walked your neighbour's children home from school for eleven years."

"And nobody thought to ask who was holding the umbrella over me. You hold one over somebody else, you get the rain down your back." The woman sniffed. "Is there any point appealing?"

Mira thought about the question properly, for the first time in a while. Not the polite version she asked eighteen times a day.

"I don't know," she said. "But I'd like to hear why it's wrong. Sit down. Tell me."

The woman sat. It took a quarter of an hour. Mira wrote down every word of it in her best hand, and read it back, and the woman nodded and said, "Yes. That's what it was like." Mira filed it. She had no idea whether it would go anywhere. She filed it anyway, and meant it.

Then she went up to the panel's office and asked for her correction back.

The clerk on duty looked at her with polite confusion. "It's in the pile for review."

"I know. I'm withdrawing it."

"On what grounds?"

"On the grounds that it's false," said Mira. "I wrote the entry it refers to, this week, in the storeroom behind Intake, in someone else's handwriting, with old ink. I'd like to put that in writing, please, with my name on it. And I'd like to ask the panel to reconvene on my mother's case as soon as it can."

The clerk stared at her. Then, very slowly, he reached for a fresh form.

Crane came down to her office within the hour. He did not stand in the doorway this time. He came in and shut the door behind him and stood in front of her desk, and for once he did not seem to know what to do with his gloves.

"You've confessed to forgery," he said.

"Yes."

"In writing. Before anyone made you."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because it was true," said Mira. "You told me you were a wall, Inspector. I've decided I don't want to be one."

He studied her, the way he might study a ring on the boundary that didn't match any key he knew.

"You understand that I'll put that confession in front of the panel," he said. "I'll argue that hiding the route makes you part of the trafficking. A beneficiary who forges a record to cover a crossing is going to look very much like a trafficker."

"I understand."

"And you still haven't given me a name."

"You'll get one," said Mira. "At the hearing. From the person it belongs to."

Crane was quiet. Then he nodded, once, very precisely, as though filing something away in that swept and numbered basement in his head.

"Then I'll see you there," he said.

That evening she climbed to the third floor with her mother's thick file under her arm, and spread it across Edmund's desk, among the pocket watches and the one-eyed doll and the music box.

"The letter is one account," Edmund said. "A good one. But the panel will set it against forty-one agreeing testimonies, and one doesn't outweigh many. That's the rule."

"I know," said Mira. "That's what I want to show you."

She took the scrap of paper out of her pocket. It had been there since the day she went down to the archive counter, folded and refolded until it was soft. Five words, underlined twice.

"Walk out into the night," Edmund read.

"Count them."

They counted. Mira read the testimonies aloud, and Edmund made a mark every time the words appeared. It took most of the evening. By the end there were thirty-six marks on his sheet of paper.

And the oldest testimony of all, the one that had formed first, was not from a person. It was from the local paper, the way an institution remembers things. A headline.

Shelter boss let vulnerable woman walk out into the night.

"They didn't remember her," said Mira. "They remembered the headline. Thirty-five people read the same sentence, and the sentence became the memory, and the memory became my mother." Her voice shook and she let it. "That isn't forty-one agreeing accounts. It's one headline, repeated thirty-five times by people who didn't know they were repeating it."

"Echoes," said Edmund softly.

"And the other five?"

The other five did not mention the night, or the woman, or the door at all. One remembered Elin's laugh, which was too loud for libraries. One remembered the tin of mints. One remembered that in thirty years she had never once locked her own front door, because, she said, you never knew who might need to come in.

Mira sat back.

Edmund was looking at her with an expression she did not recognise at first. Then she did. It was the face of someone watching another person do the thing he had stopped being able to do.

"You'll have to say all of this out loud," he said. "In that room. With Crane in the back row."

"I know," said Mira.

Outside the window, the season was beginning to turn. You could feel it in the Office the way you feel weather coming, a stillness, a change in the air. The panel had set a date. It was the last day before the inquiry closed.
