# Seed: The Worst Liar in Pennyford

## Premise
- Source: `books/collection.md` premise 4, "The liar": magic runs on stories, and a convincing lie can reshape reality. The heroine is the world's worst liar, which makes her immune to a plague of magical propaganda.
- Logline (Claude's choice, see *Author said*): *In a market town where any story a hundred people believe comes true, the one woman who has never managed a lie must make the town believe the plain truth before a charming stranger tells it out of existence.*

## Protagonist
- Name / one line: **Nell Pardy**, 19, the Market Weigher of Pennyford (keeps the town's brass scales and standard weights). Has never told a lie: when she tries, she goes red, hiccups, and the truth comes out. The town calls her "the worst liar in Pennyford", fondly.
- Want: a quiet life; to keep her scales true and not be looked at.
- Need: to say true things out loud and stand by them, even to a crowd that laughs.
- Wound: laughed at all her life for blurting the truth; she has learned to keep quiet instead, and lets the scales speak for her.
- Lie: "Nobody wants plain truth. Better to keep your mouth shut."
- Why this premise hurts her: she is the only person in town who can remember the truth, and the one who least wants to say anything to anybody.
- Opposition: **Lucan Fairweather**, a charming stranger who comes to the Michaelmas Fair and tells stories at the Half Moon that are true by morning. Really **Luke Sallow**, the tanner's boy who told whoppers and ran away from Pennyford at twelve, swearing he'd come back rich. He wants the town that forgot him to remember him as its great man.
- Supporting roles (by function):
  - **Gran** (Hester Pardy), ally and cost: raised Nell, tells small cheerful lies all day ("I'm sixty-one"), so every telling takes her. Loses her cottage to a telling. At the climax she believes Nell over her own memory.
  - **Mrs Thorne**, the Reeve, obstacle then ally: keeps the parish Book and the law; fair, needs evidence.
  - **Biscuit**, the baker's dog, comic ally and evidence: animals have never lied, so tellings don't touch him; he barks at everything a telling made.
  - Minor: Amos Bell, the old Crier (a telling turns him into a cobbler); Mr Tuttle, the baker.

## Magic implications
- The power: **a spoken story that a hundred people believe becomes true**, and every mind that has ever told a lie remembers it as always so. Rumour counts, so a good story spreads "like a cold" (the plague in the premise).
- Who profits: whoever tells the best story. Who is hurt: whoever the story is about, and everyone, since nobody can tell they've been told. Who polices it: the town charter's **Ninety-Nine** law (nobody but the sworn Crier may tell news to more than ninety-nine people at once). But a telling that takes erases its own crime, so the law only catches failures.
- Worst misuse: telling that a person is a liar, so nobody believes them again. Kindest: none in this book (parked).
- Cost: **no personal cost to the teller** (Claude's choice). The constraint is the hundred, the rule that it can't touch a body, and doubt (a telling falls when belief falls).
- Hard limit: a telling can't touch a living body, and can't change what has already happened (money paid under a telling stays paid). It holds only inside the parish where it is believed.
- Counter: doubt. A telling holds while a hundred believe it; when belief falls under a hundred it slips, the world goes back, and everyone remembers both versions. And a mind that has never lied can't be told at all, until it tells its first lie.

## Places (2–4, with smell / sound / texture)
1. **The market square and the weigh-house** (Nell's post; the Moot is held here): wet straw and apples / the clank of the brass beam settling / cold brass weights.
2. **The bridge over the Penny and its toll house** (made by the first telling): river weed / the gate's creak and pennies dropping in the box / the worn stone parapet.
3. **Gran's cottage on Ladle Lane**: onion pies and woodsmoke / the kettle and Gran humming / the warped kitchen table.
4. **The Half Moon**, the alehouse where Fairweather tells: beer and pipe smoke / laughter that stops when he talks / a sticky table.

## Set pieces (kept)
<!-- ★ = shows a rule of the magic early -->
1. Opening: a customer asks Nell if her cheese is the best at the fair; Nell tries to be polite, hiccups, and the truth comes out.
2. ★ Fairweather's first telling at the Half Moon, eighty-three listeners (Nell counts them; just under the ninety-nine), the story of the Fairweather Bridge and its penny toll.
3. ★ Morning: a toll house and a gate that weren't there last night; everyone pays as they always have; Biscuit barks at the gate.
4. ★ Gran explains never-liars over the kitchen table: "mind you never tell one, or you'll be like the rest of us."
5. ★ The Reeve's Book says the toll is two hundred years old.
6. Fairweather on the bridge, realising Nell remembers: "I've never met one of you."
7. ★ Amos Bell, the Crier turned cobbler, humming the crier's call over a boot without knowing why.
8. ★ The wobble: Nell and the Reeve talk sixty people into doubting; the toll gate goes thin as a reflection; Fairweather tells a better story and it firms again.
9. The morning Nell is the biggest liar in Pennyford: the weights taken from her, Gran's sad, loving smile.
10. The rent letter: Gran's cottage was always Mr Fairweather's.
11. ★ Fairweather's offer: one public lie, and Gran keeps her cottage. "It's lonely, isn't it, being the only one who remembers?"
12. Nell practising the lie in the dark and hiccupping.
13. Gran: "I'll go with you over my own memory."
14. The Moot: Nell breaks the Ninety-Nine to speak; the test, "lie for us, just once"; the truth comes out instead.
15. The slip: three hundred people remembering two things at once; the toll gate gone; Amos with his bell in his hand.
16. Gran gives Fairweather a pie for the road. Nell's first cry as Crier.

## Ending (chosen) and central question
- Ending (Claude's choice, warm, in line with the series' cosy tone): the tellings slip, Fairweather is shown to be Luke Sallow and leaves over the free bridge, the tolls are paid back, and the town makes Nell its Crier because she is the only one they trust to speak to more than ninety-nine. Cost to Nell: she has to do the thing she fears most, speak to everyone, and keep doing it.
- Central question: *Is a plain truth worth anything if nobody wants to hear it?*
- Motifs: the clank of the weigh-house beam ("true weight"); Nell's hiccup; the toll gate's creak and the penny in the box; Biscuit's bark; the phrase "the worst liar in Pennyford" (bad at lying; later, under the telling, "the biggest liar").

## Suggestions not yet chosen
- A dark ending: Nell tells one lie to save Gran's cottage and forgets everything, at peace, the only one who knew.
- A bittersweet ending: the tellings slip, but Gran's cottage was really sold years ago and only Fairweather's telling had kept it.

## Rejected

## Parked
- A kindly telling: a drought-year town telling itself its spring will never run dry (a nice idea for the history, but nobody could remember it as a telling, so it would need its own rules).
- The city, and outsiders crossing the boundary stones: tellings hold only inside the parish, and the book stays inside it.
