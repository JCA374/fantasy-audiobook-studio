# Chapter Three: The Crier

Monday was market day in Pennyford, and Nell Pardy watched Lucan Fairweather work the market from the weigh-house window, the way you might watch a fox work a hen run.

He never spoke to a crowd. That was the first thing she noticed. He went from stall to stall. At each one he stopped and talked to whoever was there: six people at the egg stall, a dozen round the cheese, twenty or so by the ale cart where the drovers stood. He bought something everywhere. He said everybody's name. He laughed at their jokes before they'd finished telling them. And at every stall, at some point, his voice dropped, and the people around him leaned in, and Nell knew he was telling it.

She couldn't hear the words through the glass. She didn't need to. She watched the faces.

At half past ten, Mr Tuttle came in with a sack of flour, and Nell asked him what Mr Fairweather was talking about.

"Oh, poor old Amos," said Mr Tuttle, setting the sack on the scale. "His voice. Fairweather was saying how it's gone, these last few years, and how you can't hear the news past the church any more, and how the Fairweathers used to do the crying in Pennyford, back when. It was in the family, he says. Like the bridge." He shook his head. "Sad, really. Amos was a fine Crier in his day."

"He still is the Crier, and he's not finished yet," said Nell. "I heard him on Friday. He was loud enough to rattle the window."

Mr Tuttle gave her the kind look people give you when you've said something odd at a funeral. "Twenty-two pounds," he said, nodding at the scale. "Write it down, love."

She wrote it down. Her hand was not quite steady.

***

By noon she couldn't stand it. She asked Mrs Lusk's eldest to mind the scales, which was against every rule there was, and she went out into the market after him.

She found him at the cheese stall, with a dozen people round him, Mrs Gammidge among them. His voice was low and warm, and he was saying something about how a town needed a voice it could trust. Nell stood at the edge of the group, red to the ears already, and waited for a gap. There wasn't one. He talked like water running.

"That's not true," she said, too loudly, into the middle of a sentence.

Twelve faces turned to her. Mr Fairweather turned too, and smiled, and waited, politely, as if she were a child who'd put her hand up in school.

Nell had known exactly what she was going to say. It had been clear in her head at the weigh-house. Now it all came out in the wrong order.

"He's only been here three days," she said. "Two. Two days. He came on Saturday. He hasn't. Amos Bell is the Crier. He's been the Crier for thirty-one years. His voice is fine. There's nothing wrong with it. I heard him on Friday, and the bridge was free, and this man is telling you stories, and you're going to believe them, and then they'll be true."

There was a short silence.

"Well," said Mrs Gammidge, "that's the most I've heard you say in nineteen years, Nell Pardy." Somebody laughed.

"Miss Pardy feels things very deeply," said Mr Fairweather, warmly. "It's a great quality. I wish there were more of it." And then he went on with what he had been saying, as if she had been a cart going past, and the faces turned back to him.

All but one. Mrs Gammidge was still looking at Nell. She had a small frown, and she was chewing the inside of her cheek, the way she did when she weighed up whether a customer could be trusted with credit. Nell thought she might say something.

Then Mr Fairweather said, "Martha," in that voice, and touched Mrs Gammidge's arm, and the frown went away.

Nell went back to the weigh-house. Mrs Lusk's eldest had weighed three hams and written them all in the wrong column. Nell didn't care. She sat down on her stool and counted the brass weights twice, and then a third time.

It was always like this, she thought. It had been like this since she was six. She said a true thing, and people laughed, and then they went on exactly as before, and the only difference was that she was red and they were cheerful. Gran said it was because she said true things the way other people dropped plates. Nell had decided long ago that the answer was to drop fewer plates. She had been good at it, until Saturday.

She tried to work out what she should have said instead. Something with a drowned child in it, probably. Something that made Mrs Lusk cry. But she didn't know any drowned children, and she couldn't make one up, and that, she supposed, was the whole of the problem in one sentence.

***

He came to the weigh-house at the end of the day, when the stalls were coming down and the square was full of the clatter of trestles.

He didn't come in. He leaned on the open half of the door, in the last of the light, and looked at the scales with real interest. Biscuit, under the bench outside, growled at his boots. Mr Fairweather looked down at him with a sort of amused respect.

"Your friend doesn't like me either," he said.

Nell said nothing. She was locking the weights away, and she kept her eyes on the box.

"I wanted to say," he said, "in case you were planning to run to Mrs Thorne, that I break no law, Miss Pardy. Not one. I never speak to more than ninety-nine. I count, you see. Like you." He smiled. "What people do with a story after I've told it is their own business. They tell their wives and their children and the man in the next field. That's not me. That's just a town talking to itself."

"It's still a lie," said Nell, to the weights.

"It's a story, Miss Pardy." He sounded almost hurt. "There's a difference. A lie is a small mean thing you tell to get out of trouble. A story is something people want. Nobody in that market wanted to hear that Amos Bell's voice was fine. They wanted to hear about a family that kept its promises." He paused. "Do you know what I find interesting about you, Miss Pardy?"

"I don't want to know," said Nell, which was true, and he laughed.

"I can't tell you away." He said it lightly, as if it were a curiosity he'd picked up at a fair. "I expect you've wondered. But I can't tell you out of town, or into the river, or into a fever. A telling can't touch a living body." He touched his hat. "I find that a comfort, myself. Don't you?"

Nell locked the box and looked up at last, but he had already gone.

***

On Tuesday morning she woke to the sound of a bell.

It was a big brass hand-bell, rung slowly, as Amos Bell rang it for news: three strokes, a pause, three strokes. Nell went to the window. Across the river, in the market square, there was a figure on the steps of the market cross in the Crier's blue coat, with the bell in one hand and the other hand raised.

The coat was too short in the arms. It had always been too short in the arms for Amos, who had been given it by the Crier before him. On the man wearing it now, the cuffs stopped halfway down his forearms, and his green sleeves showed underneath.

Lucan Fairweather cried the oyez across the whole of Pennyford, in a voice like warm honey.

Nell dressed so fast she put her stockings on inside out. She crossed the bridge at a run and threw her penny at the box.

On her way past the church, she heard Mrs Thorne's housekeeper talking to the milkman. The Reeve had sworn Mr Fairweather in so nicely at the spring Moot, the housekeeper was saying. She had sworn him with his hand on the Book, the way the Crier was sworn every spring, the same as his father before him. Nell had been at that Moot. Amos Bell had stood on the cross that day, with his hand on the Book. And Lucan Fairweather had come into Pennyford on the carrier's cart three days ago, a stranger, and nobody seemed to mind that both things were true.

She went straight past the market cross without looking at him, down Shambles Row to the little house where Amos Bell lived.

There was a new sign above Amos's door. It was painted wood, a picture of a boot, and it said, A. Bell, Boots Mended. Under it, through the open half of the door, Amos Bell sat at a cobbler's last with a boot upside down on it and a mouthful of tacks.

He was seventy and stooped, and he had a voice that could carry over a cattle fair. Nell had heard it every week of her life. He looked up at her and took the tacks out of his mouth.

He nodded at her feet, and his voice filled the little room the way it had always filled the square. "Morning, Nell. You're out early. Is it a heel come off, or a strap?"

"Amos," she said. "Amos, how long have you been a cobbler?"

He looked at her oddly. "Since I was fourteen. Same as my father. You know that."

She didn't know that. She knew he'd been the Crier for thirty-one years. She knew he'd had the bell from old Crier Pask, who'd had it from somebody else. She knew he polished it on Sunday nights with a rag and a tin of brass polish, because Gran had told her so. She looked at his hands. They were holding the hammer wrong. He held it the way you'd hold a bell: loosely, from the top, the wrist ready to swing.

He went back to the boot. After a moment, under his breath, he began to hum.

It was the Crier's call. The old tune he'd always whistled before the bell, the one Mr Fairweather had whistled on the bridge. Amos hummed it over the boot without seeming to know that he was doing it. He tapped the tacks in to the same beat: one, two, three, then a pause, then one, two, three.

Out in the square, Biscuit had started barking. Nell didn't need to look to know what at. He was barking at the blue coat on the wrong man.

"Oyez," came the honey voice again, over the roofs. "Hear the news, Pennyford! The Autumn Moot will be held in this square, three weeks from Saturday last. And there," said Lucan Fairweather, and the noise of the market dropped away under his voice, "I shall tell this town the story it deserves."

Eighteen days, Nell thought. She counted them twice to be sure.

Then she went to find Mrs Thorne, who wanted evidence, and who now remembered swearing him in herself, at the spring Moot. Nell had no evidence at all. She knocked anyway.
