# Outline: The Worst Liar in Pennyford

## Throughlines

### Act I: The Toll

**1. Short Weight** (1,900 words). Michaelmas Fair, Saturday.
- Opening: the weigh-house at the fair. Mrs Gammidge asks Nell if her cheese isn't the best at the fair; Nell tries to say yes, goes red, hiccups, "It's sweaty." Laughter: "the worst liar in Pennyford". Biscuit under the bench for crusts.
- Nell's world fast: the beam, the brass weights, Gran's pie stall, Nell counting things to keep calm.
- A stranger in a green coat with silver buttons, Lucan Fairweather, buys Gran's last six pies and remembers every name. Charming; Nell distrusts him for no reason she could weigh.
- Evening: Nell carries Gran's pie tray to the Half Moon. Fairweather is buying a round; the landlord counts heads, and Fairweather stops him at ninety-nine with a wink ("we keep the law in here"). Nell counts eighty-three.
- The telling: the Fairweather Bridge, built by his great-great-grandfather after the flood, its penny toll kept faithfully for two hundred years, how his family let Pennyford forget out of kindness. Beautiful. Nell thinks: the bridge is free, I walked over it this morning. Others nod, moved. "Tell it at home, friends. Let it catch."
- Night: Nell walks home over the free bridge, cold stone, nobody there.
- Rules: 1, 2, 11 (the ninety-nine), hint of 8.
- Closing hook: Sunday dawn, Nell walking to church, and a toll house with a creaking gate at the end of the bridge, Mr Tuttle paying his penny, chatting, as he always has. Biscuit barking at the gate.

**2. The Toll** (1,900 words). Sunday.
- Nell: "That wasn't there yesterday." Mr Tuttle laughs: it's been there all his life. The toll keeper, a Fairweather man, takes her penny; she pays because everyone is looking. Her first scene of "say nothing".
- Gran's kitchen: Nell tells her. Gran remembers paying the toll as a girl. Then Gran goes still: "You've never told a lie, have you, pet. Not one." Never-liars (rules 8–10): babies, animals, and Nell. "Mind you never do, or you'll be like the rest of us." Gran tells of a telling that slipped when she was a girl visiting her aunt in the next parish: everyone remembered both, and the money paid stayed paid (rules 5, 6). Gran believes in tellings; she can't believe this one is one: her memory is too sure.
- The Reeve's house: Mrs Thorne and the Book. "Toll granted to the Fairweather family, in the year seventeen hundred and twelve." The Book is paper (13). The Ninety-Nine (11); he kept to it (12). "Bring me two witnesses." Nell: Biscuit. Mrs Thorne half laughs: dogs can't swear.
- Dusk: the bridge. Fairweather leaning on the parapet. He's friendly, curious; "You paid with a face like thunder, Miss Pardy." She tells him it's not true. He studies her, delighted and wary: "I've never met one of you."
- Rules: 3, 5, 6 (told), 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13.
- Closing hook: he tips his hat: "I wonder how long you'll keep it up," and walks off whistling the old crier's call.

### Act II-A: The Crier

**3. The Crier** (1,900 words). Monday–Tuesday.
- Market day. Nell at the weigh-house watches Fairweather work the stalls, a group at a time, never more than a few dozen: a story about Amos Bell's tired voice, and the Fairweathers who always cried the news.
- Nell tries to follow and contradict. She's terrible: tongue-tied, red, numbers instead of stories ("He's been here three days. Two. Two days."). Laughter. One woman, Mrs Gammidge, hesitates. Not enough.
- Fairweather, quietly, at the weigh-house: "I break no law, Miss Pardy." And "I can't tell you away, you know. Bodies are out of bounds. I find that a comfort, don't you?" (rules 4, 12).
- Tuesday dawn: Fairweather in the crier's coat on the market cross, ringing a bell. Amos Bell at a cobbler's last, humming the crier's call over a boot without knowing why. His hands. Biscuit barks at the coat.
- Fairweather cries the Autumn Moot, three weeks off, "when I'll tell Pennyford the story it deserves."
- Rules: 2, 3, 4, 12.
- Closing hook: Nell decides she has to do something, and goes to the one person who says "evidence": Mrs Thorne.

**4. Thin as Glass** (1,900 words). The following Saturday (midpoint).
- Mrs Thorne listens. She doesn't remember, but she knows Nell: "You couldn't tell me a cake was nice when it wasn't." She agrees to stand with her at the bridge on Saturday and keep the count under ninety-nine.
- Saturday at the bridge, sixty people. Nell speaks badly, then well: plain details (the toll house has no moss; the gate's hinges are new; Mr Tuttle has never once mentioned paying in nineteen years). Mrs Thorne says: "I have never known Nell Pardy tell a lie."
- Doubt catches: the gate goes thin as a reflection, the pennies in the box pale; Mrs Thorne almost remembers walking over with no penny (rules 6, 7).
- Fairweather arrives, unhurried, and tells them a better story: the flood, the drowned, his ancestor's promise. Their eyes go soft. The gate firms. Mrs Thorne blinks: "Where was I?"
- Night: Nell at the Half Moon window. Fairweather inside, eighty-odd faces, telling a story about a girl who never told the truth in her life.
- Rules: 6, 7.
- Closing hook: "Nell Pardy," he was saying, "the biggest liar in Pennyford."

### Act II-B: The Biggest Liar

**5. The Biggest Liar** (1,900 words). Sunday–Monday.
- Morning: Mr Tuttle won't meet her eye. Church: people move along the pew. She is still herself (rule 4): same hands, same hiccup. Only their memories have changed: short weight, tall tales, she told the vicar his wife was sweet on the curate.
- Monday: Mrs Thorne, kind and firm, takes the weigh-house key and the brass weights. "I've overlooked it for years, Nell." Nell counts the weights into the box, all sixteen.
- Gran: sad, loving: "You've always told your stories, pet. I don't mind." Worse than anger. Nell: "I've never lied." Gran pats her hand.
- The rent letter: to Hester Pardy, tenant, from L. Fairweather, owner of Ladle Lane: the quarter's rent overdue, three pounds. Gran remembers paying him every quarter. The Reeve will enforce it (rule 15).
- Rules: 4, 8, 15.
- Closing hook: a knock at the door, and the voice like warm honey: "Mrs Pardy. Might I have a word with your granddaughter?"

**6. The Offer** (1,900 words). Mid-week before the Moot.
- Fairweather in Gran's kitchen, cap in hand, eating a pie. He sends Gran out on an errand with a kind lie. Alone with Nell: the offer. At the Moot, stand up and say "I made the free bridge up." One lie. He tears up the rent and Gran keeps her cottage for life. "And you'd forget. It's lonely, isn't it, being the only one who remembers?" (rules 9, 10, 16). He tells her his plan for the Moot: the founding story, every stone.
- After he goes: Gran back, chatting, mentions the tanner's boy, Luke Sallow, who told whoppers and swore he'd come back rich: "had a way of saying your name like he'd bought it." Nell hears it. The same habit: Fairweather says everyone's name. Gran has never connected them.
- Night: Nell practises the lie in the dark: "I made the free bridge up." Hiccup. Again. The third time, it nearly comes out clean, and she is frightened at how close it was. All is lost: she will do it, for Gran.
- Gran in the doorway with a candle, having heard. "Is it true, what you say about the bridge?" Nell, after a long silence: "Yes." Gran: her memory says Nell's a liar, but "I've watched you go red over a burnt pie for nineteen years." "I'll go with you over my own memory." Nell: she'll speak at the Moot.
- Rules: 9, 10, 16.
- Closing hook: Gran: "Then we'd best think what to say, because you're the worst teller of a story I ever met."

### Act III: The Moot

**7. The Moot** (1,900 words). The Autumn Moot.
- The square full: about three hundred. Fairweather on the market cross in the crier's coat. Nell on the weigh-house steps, counting.
- Before he starts the founding story, Nell speaks, breaking the Ninety-Nine. Jeers: the biggest liar. Mrs Thorne orders her down. Gran: "If she's the biggest liar in Pennyford, let her lie for us."
- Fairweather takes it up, smiling, because he sees the trap working for him either way: "Lie for us, then, Miss Pardy. Just once. Tell them I'm an honest man." If she can, she forgets everything (rule 9).
- Nell tries, for them. Red, hiccups. "You're Luke Sallow. You're the tanner's boy. And you're a thief." Laughter, then quiet. Mr Tuttle: "That's Nell. That's just like Nell."
- Fairweather tells it again, fast and beautiful: she's practised that trick for years (rule 7). Some go soft-eyed. Gran: "Then do it again, pet." Mrs Thorne asks her to say the cake was nice. She can't. Each failure is something three hundred memories can't explain (rule 6).
- Nell counts the faces turning: doubt passing like a cold. The weigh-house weights in the new weigher's hand go thin.
- Rules: 11, 6, 7, 8, 9 at work.
- Closing hook: the telling slips, and every person in the square remembers two things at once.

**8. True Weight** (1,900 words). The Moot and the morning after.
- The slip, from Nell's eyes: people blinking at her, remembering that she never lied, that they were told, and who told them. "He told us."
- The cascade: knowing they were told once, they doubt everything he said. The crier's coat hangs loose; Amos Bell finds his bell in his hand; down the street the toll gate creaks once and is gone (rules 6, 14). The rent.
- Fairweather on the cross, telling one last story, and nobody believes it; just a man in a green coat. Mrs Thorne: banishment from the parish for false telling to more than ninety-nine, now proved; every toll repaid from the box (rules 11, 14, 5).
- Nell speaks for him, plainly: tells the town who he is, Luke Sallow, the boy they laughed at, so they remember him truly. He looks at her as if she has hit him and thanked him at once.
- Nell's fine for breaking the Ninety-Nine: Mrs Thorne sets it at one penny, "the toll you never paid". Nell pays it.
- Dawn: Fairweather walks out over the free bridge; Gran gives him a pie for the road. Biscuit crosses without barking.
- Mrs Thorne asks Nell to be the Crier: the only voice the town can trust with more than ninety-nine. Nell's first cry, plain and short. The weigh-house beam clanks in the square: true weight.
- Rules: 6, 14, 5, 11 paid off.
- Last line: the worst liar in Pennyford, telling the truth to all of it.
