# Chapter Six: The Offer

Lucan Fairweather sat at Gran's warped kitchen table in Ladle Lane with his hat on his knee. He was eating one of Gran's onion pies, and he told her it was the best pie he'd had since the fair.

Gran went pink. Nell, standing by the hob with her arms folded, watched her go pink and wanted to throw the kettle at him.

"I won't keep you long, Mrs Pardy," he said. "It's only a small matter between your granddaughter and me. Oh, and I passed Mrs Lusk on the corner, and she was asking for you. Something about her youngest and a cough. I said I'd tell you."

"Oh, the poor lamb," said Gran, and had her shawl on and was out of the door before Nell could say a word.

Nell looked at him.

"Mrs Lusk's youngest hasn't got a cough," she said.

"No," Mr Fairweather agreed. "But your grandmother will have a nice chat on the corner, and Mrs Lusk will give her a jar of something, and they'll both come home happy." He brushed crumbs off his fingers. "That's the thing you've never understood, Miss Pardy. Most lies aren't wicked. Most of them are just a way of being kind quickly."

He waited, as if he thought she might argue. She didn't. She had heard Gran tell a hundred of that sort, and she had never once been able to explain to herself what was wrong with them.

"I'll come to the point," he said, "since I don't think you like it when people don't. In twelve days there's a Moot. The whole parish, in the square. I'll be on the cross. And before I begin, I'd like you to stand up, in front of everyone, and say one sentence for me." He held up a finger. "Just this, in your own voice: I made the free bridge up."

Nell stared at him.

"That's all. One sentence. You'd be telling the town you lied to them about the bridge, and they'd all nod, because they already think so." He smiled. "And in return, I'll tear up your grandmother's rent letter in front of you. Better than that. I'll put this cottage in her name in the Book, for her lifetime, at a peppercorn rent. She'll never see another letter from me as long as she lives. And until the Moot, I'll tell the bailiff to wait."

"Why?" said Nell. Her mouth was dry. "Why do you care what I say? Everyone already thinks I'm a liar. You made sure of that."

"Because I don't like loose ends. And because you're the only one in this town who can see the joins." He turned his hat round on his knee. "Oh, I could tell them you'd been banished, I suppose. They'd walk you to the boundary stones, and you'd stand there for the rest of your life, telling every carter who came past. No. I'd much rather you were one of us. You'll be watching me at the Moot. And I've seen what happens to a crowd when you start pointing at roofs."

He leaned forward. His voice dropped, and Nell found herself leaning in to hear it, and hated herself.

"Here's the part I think you'll like," he said. "You'd forget. You're a never-liar, Miss Pardy. Your grandmother must have told you what happens the first time a never-liar tells a lie. You'd be like everyone else, the moment the words left your mouth. You'd remember the toll and the Crier and the rent, all as they've always been. You'd remember being a little wild as a girl, and growing out of it, and being sorry. You'd have your grandmother's cottage, and your grandmother's good opinion, and you'd never know you'd lost a thing." He sat back. "It's lonely, isn't it? Being the only one who remembers."

The kettle muttered. Nell didn't say anything. She couldn't, because it was true, and she had never been able to say anything else.

"Think it over, Miss Pardy. Twelve days. And do thank your grandmother for the pie," he said, and stood, and put on his hat.

"What are you going to tell them?" Nell asked. "At the Moot. What story?"

He paused in the doorway. Just then, he didn't look pleasant at all. He looked tired, and older than she'd thought.

"The founding of Pennyford," he said. "Who built it. Who it belongs to. Every stone. A story that size won't catch in alehouses, Miss Pardy. It needs the whole town at once, from the cross, from the Crier." He smiled again, and the pleasant face came back. "A town ought to remember who made it, Miss Pardy. Don't you think?"

***

Gran came home half an hour later with a jar of Mrs Lusk's damson jam and a puzzled face.

"She said the little one was right as rain," said Gran, hanging up her shawl. "I must have got the wrong end of it. Still, we had a nice chat." She put the jam on the table. "Has he gone? Lovely manners, that man. He put me in mind of someone, sitting there. I've been trying to think who, all the way down the lane."

Nell waited, and didn't help her.

"Luke Sallow," said Gran, as if it had just come to her. "The tanner's boy. Ruth Sallow's lad, from down by the old tan-yard. Oh, you won't remember him, pet, he was gone before you were born." She sat down and rubbed her knees. "Poor little scrap. Thin as a rake, smelled of the tan pits, and he told the most terrible whoppers. He told the whole school his uncle was a lord with a carriage. He told the curate he'd seen an angel on the church roof. Nobody believed a word of it, of course. They laughed at him something cruel. He'd stand in the middle of the playground and tell his stories with his chin up, and the bigger lads would laugh till they cried." She shook her head. "And he had a way of saying your name. Every time he spoke to you. As if he'd bought it off you and wanted you to know it was his now."

Nell went very still.

"What happened to him?" Nell's hands had gone cold.

"Ran off. Twelve years old. Stood on the bridge one morning and told everyone who'd listen that he was going to the city, and he'd come back rich, and then we'd all be sorry." Gran gave a little laugh. "And off he went, and nobody ever heard of him again. Ruth died a year or two after. The tan-yard's nettles now." She reached for the jam. "Nobody thought about him much, poor mite. I suppose that was always his trouble."

Nell did the sum in her head. He had been twelve, and gone before she was born. He would be thirty-five now, or near enough.

Luke. Lucan.

A town ought to remember who made it.

***

She lay awake that night until the church clock struck one. Then she got up, quietly, and lit a candle, and stood in front of the little square of mirror on her bedroom wall.

She was going to have to practise. She knew that much. If she stood up in front of three hundred people and went red and hiccupped and couldn't get it out, Gran would lose the cottage anyway, and she would have shamed them both for nothing.

"I made the free bridge up," she whispered to the mirror.

Her neck went hot. Her ears went hot. The frog jumped in her chest, and she hiccupped so loudly she clapped her hand over her mouth.

She waited until her heart slowed. Then she tried again.

"I made the free," she said, and hiccupped, and the rest came out in a rush, "the bridge was free, it was always free, there wasn't any gate."

The truth. As always. Falling out of her like plates off a shelf.

She stood and breathed for a long time. Then she thought about Gran, smoothing the rent letter flat on the table with both hands. She thought about Gran out on the lane with her chair and her pie tins and her kettle, and the name gone from over the back door. She thought: it's one sentence. It's only one. Then I won't know, and I won't mind, and it will all be so much easier.

"I made the free bridge up," she said to the mirror.

It came out nearly clean. There was only a little catch in it, a tiny hitch on the last word, hardly a hiccup at all. Her face in the glass was red, but the words had come out whole, one after the other, in the right order.

Nell sat down on the bed, hard, and put her hands over her face. She was shaking. She had never been so frightened in her life. Not at the bridge. Not in the market. Not at the window of the Half Moon. It had been so close. One more try, and the words would come out clean. Then, at the Moot, they would go through, and afterwards she wouldn't even know.

Gran said her name from the doorway, very quietly.

Nell looked up. Gran was standing at the top of the stairs in her nightgown, with a candle of her own and her grey plait over her shoulder. Nell had no idea how long she'd been there.

"Is it true?" said Gran quietly. "What you say about the bridge. Is it true, pet?"

Nell opened her mouth. Nothing came out, true or otherwise. Then she said, "Yes."

Gran came in and sat down on the bed beside her. The candle wobbled in her hand. She didn't say anything for a while.

"My memory says you're a liar," she said at last. "It's as clear as anything. I can see you at six, telling me about the man in the well. I can see you at twelve, telling Mrs Gammidge her cheese was off, when it was the best on the stall. I can see all of it." She looked at Nell. "And I've just stood on those stairs for the best part of an hour, listening to you try to tell one lie, and you can't. You can't get it out. You go red, and you hiccup, and the truth comes out instead, the way it did when you were three." She put the candle down on the floor. "Liars don't practise, pet. They don't need to. I should know."

Nell started to cry. She hadn't cried, now she thought about it, since she was about nine.

Gran put an arm round her. She smelled of onions and woodsmoke, the same as always.

"I've known you nineteen years," said Gran into her hair. "I've watched you go red over a burnt pie. I'll go with you over my own memory." She sniffed. "Now. Is this about the cottage? Because if that man thinks I'd have you tell a lie to keep a roof over my head, he doesn't know me. I've slept in worse places than a hedge."

"He's Luke Sallow," said Nell, into Gran's shoulder. "The tanner's boy. It's him."

Gran was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, softly, "Oh, the poor little devil." And then, in quite a different voice, "Well, he's not having my house."

"I'm going to speak at the Moot," said Nell. "I don't know what I'll say. But I'm going to say it to all of them."

Gran held her a little away and looked at her, and her eyes were wet and bright.

"Then we'd best start thinking what to say," said Gran. "Because I love you, pet, but you're the worst teller of a story I ever met."
