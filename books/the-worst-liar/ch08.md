# Chapter Eight: True Weight

Nell Pardy saw it happen from the weigh-house steps. Afterwards, when people asked her what it had looked like, she could only say that it looked like three hundred people waking up at once.

Nobody fell over. Nobody cried out. It was quieter than that. All over the market square, faces went blank for a moment, as if their owners had walked into a room and forgotten what they came in for. Then the faces changed. Mrs Gammidge put her hand to her mouth. The curate sat down on the edge of the horse trough. Mr Hobday, in the doorway behind Nell, said "Oh," softly, and set the box of weights down on the step.

Nell knew what they were remembering, because they started to say it out loud.

"She never lied," said Mrs Lusk. She was looking at Nell, and her eyes were full. "Not ever. She told my Annie her singing was flat, at the harvest supper, and Annie cried for a week. She never told a lie in her life." She turned to the woman beside her. "And I remember she did. Both. I remember both."

"He told us," said Mr Tuttle. He said it slowly, like a man working out a sum. "At the Half Moon. The night of the meeting at the bridge. He stood by the fire and told us she'd been lying since she could talk, and I went home and told my wife." He looked down at Biscuit, who was sitting on Nell's boots. "And the dog wouldn't go near him."

It went through the square the way the doubt had, from mouth to mouth, but faster now. He told us. He told us at the Half Moon. He told us at the cheese stall. He told us at the bridge. People turned to each other and said it, and as they said it, Nell saw them begin to think the next thing. If he had told them that, what else had he told them?

Then they began to look around.

Nell heard it before she saw it. Down at the bottom of the square, where the lane ran down to the river, there was a long, slow creak, the sound the toll gate made when somebody pushed through it. It creaked once, all on its own, with nobody near it. And then it didn't creak again, because it wasn't there.

People ran to look. Nell didn't need to. She could see the end of the bridge from the weigh-house steps. There was the lamp on its post, and the black shapes of the willows, and the road running straight on over the worn stones. There was no toll house. There had never been a toll house. Everybody in Pennyford remembered that now. They also remembered paying a penny to cross the bridge every day for three weeks, both at once, and the two memories sat side by side in their heads like a pair of odd boots.

At the edge of the crowd, someone was pushing through. It was Amos Bell, in his shirtsleeves, with his old hands empty. He went up the steps of the market cross, slowly, because he was seventy. At the top he stopped in front of Lucan Fairweather and held out his hand.

Lucan Fairweather looked at him. Then he gave Amos the bell.

Amos took it as Nell had seen him hold the cobbler's hammer: loosely, from the top, with his wrist ready to swing. He didn't ring it. He just stood and held it, and his old face crumpled, and he said, to no one, "Thirty-one years."

***

Lucan Fairweather tried once more. Nell had known he would.

He spread his hands on the steps of the cross, in the blue coat that was too short in the arms, and he began to tell them something. It was about a misunderstanding. It was about a family that had done its best. It was beautiful, probably. Nell didn't listen, and neither did anybody else. Three hundred people stood in the square and looked at him, and not one of them leaned in. After a while he stopped, in the middle of a sentence, and there was nothing but the wind and somebody's baby crying near the cheese stall.

He was just a man in a green coat, standing on some steps.

Mrs Thorne opened the Book. Nell saw her turn the pages and stop, and saw her mouth go tight. Whatever had been written about the Fairweather toll had gone. The page was the same as it had been before the fair. She shut the Book carefully and stood up straight.

"Luke Sallow," she said, and the whole square heard her. "That is your name. Today, before more than ninety-nine people, as the false Crier of this town, you told this parish a story you knew to be false. I never swore you in. I remember that now, as well as I remember swearing Amos. Every person in this square remembers it. That is the proof the law needs." She took a breath. "You are banished from the parish of Pennyford, from sunrise tomorrow, and you will not cross the boundary stones again. Before you go, you will pay back every penny that was paid at that gate. Nell will do the counting."

Luke Sallow nodded. He didn't argue. He took off the blue coat, folded it, and laid it over the rail of the cross. Then he took a heavy leather purse out of his own coat and held it out over the edge of the steps, and after a moment Mr Hobday went and took it.

The man in the red scarf was nowhere to be seen. Somebody said afterwards that he had been seen going up the Hollin road at a trot, before the square had even finished remembering. Nobody on Ladle Lane owed anybody any rent. Nobody ever had, and now they all knew it.

Nell had called him a thief, and he was one. But Gran had said something else about him too, on the bed, in the dark: oh, the poor little devil. Both things were true. Nell had been thinking about that while Mrs Thorne spoke. She was surprised to find it was still in her head, and that it wanted to come out. It usually did. This time she didn't try to stop it.

"Mrs Thorne," she said. "Can I say something? To everyone?"

Mrs Thorne looked at her. "You've already broken the Ninety-Nine once today, Nell," she said. "I don't suppose twice will hang you."

Nell stood on the weigh-house steps and looked out at all of them. Three hundred and nine, if the Hobday boys were counted. Her neck went hot. She let it.

"He's Luke Sallow," she said. "I said that before, but I said it like a bad thing, and I didn't mean all of it. He's Ruth Sallow's boy. He grew up at the tan-yard, and he told stories, and you laughed at him. Gran says he'd stand in the middle of the playground with his chin up and tell them, and the big lads laughed till they cried. And then he went away, and nobody thought about him again." She swallowed. "That's all. I just think, if we're going to remember him, we should remember all of it. Not just today."

The square was very quiet.

On the cross, Luke Sallow was looking at her. Nell had never seen anyone look like that before. It was as if she had slapped him and thanked him at the same time, and he didn't know which to answer first.

Then Mrs Thorne cleared her throat and said, "And the fine for breaking the Ninety-Nine, twice, Nell Pardy, is one penny. The price of crossing that bridge, for three weeks." And somebody laughed, the fond kind, and then a lot of people did, and Nell went red, and dug in her pocket, and paid it.

***

Luke Sallow left Pennyford at sunrise the next morning, over the bridge, on foot, with a bag over his shoulder.

Nell was there. So was Gran, in her shawl, with a covered basket on her arm. So was Biscuit, who had come because Nell had come. Gran's grandfather's name was back over her back door, carved deep into the old oak with the year beside it, and she had got up three times in the night to go and touch it.

The bridge was empty from end to end. The stone parapet was rough and damp under Nell's hand, the same as every morning of her life. The river talked to itself below.

Luke Sallow stopped when he saw them. He looked thinner without the blue coat, and younger, and very tired.

"Mrs Pardy," he said. Then he stopped, and didn't say her name a second time. "Miss Pardy."

Gran held out the basket. "Onion," she said. "Two of them. You'll be hungry by Hollin."

He took it. For a moment it looked as if he might say something beautiful. Then he didn't. He just said, "Thank you," in a perfectly ordinary voice, and it was the first thing Nell had ever heard him say that she was sure he meant.

"Was any of it true?" Nell asked. "Any of the stories?"

He thought about it. She could see him deciding to tell her the truth, and how strange it felt to him.

"The drowned children," he said. "At the old ford, before the bridge. My mother told me about them when I was small. That part was true." He looked down at the water. "I just gave them a better bridge."

Then he touched his hat to them both and walked away over the free stones, and didn't pay anybody anything. Biscuit trotted halfway across the bridge after him, and stopped, and sat down, and didn't bark at all.

***

Mrs Thorne gave Nell back the weigh-house key the day after the Moot, and counted the sixteen brass weights into her hands herself. On the next market day, Amos Bell came to the weigh-house, where Nell was back on her stool. He put the Crier's bell down on her counter, beside the scale.

"My voice is fine," he said. "Whatever he told them. But I'm seventy, Nell, and my knees aren't, and that bell gets heavier every winter. I'd like to sit down." He nodded at the bell. "The Crier swears to cry nothing but the truth. The Reeve and I have been talking. We can't think of anyone who'd find that easier."

Nell looked at the bell for a long time. She could feel her neck getting hot just thinking about it. Three hundred people. Every week.

Then she picked it up.

She stood on the steps of the market cross that noon, in a blue coat that was too short in the arms on her too, and rang the bell. Three strokes, a pause, three strokes. The market went quiet. All over the square, faces turned to her, and waited.

She didn't have a story. She had never had a story in her life. She had four pieces of news, and she said them plainly, in the order they'd happened, without a single drowned child in any of them. The bridge was free. Mr Hobday's pig had got out again. The Moot had voted to mend the church roof. And Mrs Thorne's lemon cake, at the harvest supper, would be dry.

Nobody leaned in. They didn't need to. Everyone could hear her perfectly well.

Behind her, in the weigh-house, somebody put a sack of flour on the scale, and the brass beam settled with a soft clank. True weight.

And the worst liar in Pennyford rang her bell again, and told the whole town the truth.
