## Premise & Theme

### Core Premise
In Pennyford, a market town on a small river called the Penny, a story that a hundred people believe becomes true, and everyone who has ever told a lie remembers it as always so. Everyone has told a lie, except Nell Pardy, the Market Weigher, who has never managed one in her life. At the Michaelmas Fair a charming stranger, Lucan Fairweather, buys a round at the Half Moon and tells a story about the bridge. By morning the bridge has a toll gate, and Nell is the only person in town who remembers it was free.

### Thematic Question
Is a plain truth worth anything if nobody wants to hear it?

### Moral Argument
A good story will always be easier to believe than a plain truth, and keeping quiet hands the town to whoever tells the best one. The truth only counts when someone says it out loud and stands by it. Nell learns to speak; the town learns that the plain, awkward voice is the one to trust.

### Recurring Motifs
- **The weigh-house beam.** The clank of the brass beam settling is "true weight". Nell counts things: weights, people, pennies. At the climax she counts the doubters.
- **The hiccup.** Whenever Nell tries to lie she goes red, hiccups, and the truth comes out. Comic in ch1, a trap in ch6, the proof in ch7.
- **The toll gate's creak and the penny in the box.** The first thing a telling made, and the last thing to go.
- **Biscuit's bark.** The baker's dog barks at everything a telling made, because animals have never lied.
- **"The worst liar in Pennyford."** A fond nickname (bad at lying) that the telling turns into "the biggest liar in Pennyford". The last line takes it back.

## Plot Foundation

### Three-Act Structure

**Act I: The Toll** (ch1–2)
- Ordinary world: the Michaelmas Fair, the weigh-house, Gran's pies, a town that laughs at Nell kindly.
- Inciting incident: Fairweather's story at the Half Moon (ch1). Next morning the toll gate is there, and Nell alone remembers.
- Response: Nell tells Gran and the Reeve. Gran explains never-liars; the Book says the toll is two hundred years old; the Reeve wants two witnesses. Fairweather learns that Nell remembers (end ch2).

**Act II-A: The Crier** (ch3–4)
- Fairweather tells his second story through the market, a group at a time, and by morning he is Pennyford's Crier; old Amos Bell is a cobbler (ch3). Nell tries to contradict him at the stalls and is laughed at.
- Midpoint (ch4): Nell and Mrs Thorne talk sixty people at the bridge into doubting. The toll gate goes thin as a reflection. Fairweather arrives and tells them a better story, and it firms. That night he tells the Half Moon a story about Nell.

**Act II-B: The Biggest Liar** (ch5–6)
- Ch5: everyone remembers Nell as the biggest liar in Pennyford. The Reeve takes her weights. Gran loves her anyway, sadly. Then the rent letter: Gran's cottage has always been Mr Fairweather's.
- Ch6: Fairweather's offer: tell one lie at the Moot ("I made the free bridge up") and Gran keeps her cottage; Nell would forget, and be at peace. Gran's memories of Luke Sallow, the tanner's boy, tell Nell who he is. Nell practises the lie in the dark and nearly manages it. All is lost, then Gran's choice: "I'll go with you over my own memory."

**Act III: The Moot** (ch7–8)
- Climax (ch7): the Autumn Moot. Fairweather on the market cross, about to tell the whole parish that his family founded Pennyford. Nell breaks the Ninety-Nine and speaks. "Lie for us, then. Just once." She can't. Doubt spreads, and the story about her slips.
- Resolution (ch8): the cascade. The toll gate, the crier's coat and the rent all go back. Fairweather is Luke Sallow again in everyone's memory; the Reeve banishes him and makes him pay back every toll. Gran gives him a pie for the road. The town makes Nell its Crier.

## Character Registry

### Protagonist: Nell Pardy
- Age: nineteen. Market Weigher of Pennyford for two years (the youngest ever; the old Weigher picked her "because she can't fib about a quarter-ounce").
- Physical: tall, big hands, freckles, goes red from the neck up. Brown hair tied back with string.
- Want: a quiet life; to keep the scales true and not be looked at.
- Need: to say true things out loud and stand by them.
- Wound: laughed at all her life for blurting the truth (at six she told the vicar his sermon was long, in church). She has learned that the safe thing is to say nothing.
- Lie: "Nobody wants plain truth. Better to keep your mouth shut."
- Arc: positive. From the weigh-house where the scales speak for her, to the market cross speaking for the town.
- Voice: short, plain sentences. Numbers. "That's not true" without softening. When she tries to be polite and it isn't true, she hiccups, and the truth comes out anyway. Counts under her breath when nervous.
- Never-liar: she has tried. It never comes out. Gran says that makes her the only person in Pennyford a telling can't reach.

### Opposition: Lucan Fairweather (really Luke Sallow)
- Age: about thirty-five. Green coat, silver buttons, a voice like warm honey, remembers everyone's name after hearing it once.
- Want: to be Pennyford's great man; the town that forgot him should remember him as its founder's heir.
- Truth: Luke Sallow, the tanner's boy, who told whoppers, was laughed at, and ran off at twelve swearing he'd come back rich. He did, in the cities, by telling.
- Method: never speaks to more than ninety-nine at once (the law); tells a story in a room, lets it catch overnight. Charming, never cruel to anyone's face. He likes Nell, in a way, and is honestly curious about her.
- Voice: "Friends," long rolling sentences, questions he answers himself, every listener's name. Never says anything plainly when he could say it beautifully.
- His tellings, in order: (1) the bridge was always his family's toll bridge (ch1); (2) he has always been Pennyford's Crier (ch3); (3) Nell Pardy is the biggest liar in Pennyford (ch4–5); (4) the Ladle Lane cottages were always his (ch5); (5, planned for the Moot) the Fairweathers founded Pennyford and own every stone of it.

### Supporting Cast
- **Gran (Hester Pardy)**, seventy-four (says sixty-one). Bakes onion pies for the fair. Raised Nell after Nell's mother died. Tells small cheerful lies all day ("I'm sixty-one", "I never touched your apple"), so every telling takes her. Says "pet". Remembers Luke Sallow. Function: ally and cost.
- **Mrs Thorne (Margery Thorne)**, the Reeve: keeps the parish Book and the law. Brisk, fair, "Evidence, Nell." Fond of Nell because Nell can't flatter. Function: obstacle, then ally. A telling takes her too.
- **Biscuit**, the baker's dog: brown, barrel-shaped, sits outside the weigh-house for crusts. Barks at the toll gate, at the crier's coat on the wrong man, at anything a telling made. Function: evidence nobody can swear to.
- **Amos Bell**, the old Crier (minor): seventy, bell-voiced. A telling makes him a cobbler. His hands still remember the bell.
- **Mr Tuttle**, the baker (minor): Biscuit's owner; loud, kind, first in every crowd.

## World Details

### Setting
**Pennyford**: a market town of about three hundred souls on the River Penny. One stone bridge, a market square with a stone cross and the weigh-house, a church, the Half Moon alehouse, Ladle Lane of small cottages. The parish ends at the boundary stones. No city in sight; the book stays inside the parish.

Time: from the Michaelmas Fair (end of September) to the Autumn Moot three weeks later.

### Sensory Palette (by ear)
- **Market square and weigh-house:** wet straw and apples / the clank of the brass beam settling / cold brass weights.
- **The bridge and toll house:** river weed / the gate's creak, pennies dropping in the box / the worn stone parapet.
- **Gran's cottage, Ladle Lane:** onion pies and woodsmoke / the kettle and Gran humming / the warped kitchen table.
- **The Half Moon:** beer and pipe smoke / laughter that stops when he talks / sticky tables.

### Social/Political Context
A small town that governs itself: the Reeve keeps the Book and the law, the Crier speaks for the town, the Moot (the whole parish in the square) meets twice a year. Everyone has heard of tellings as one hears of plague: things that happen in cities. The Ninety-Nine law exists because of them, and nobody in Pennyford has seen one in living memory.

## Magic: Tellings
- Source / how it works: words spoken and believed change the world. When a hundred people believe a spoken story at once, it becomes true, and every mind that has ever lied remembers it as always so. Nobody knows why. Grandmothers say the world is lazy and would rather agree with people than argue.
- Who can use it: anyone who can make a hundred people believe them. Most stories never get near a hundred; good tellers are rare and dangerous.
- Cost to user: none (Claude's choice). The constraints are the hundred, the body, the past, the parish, and doubt.
- Hard limits: can't touch a living body; can't change what has happened; holds only in the parish where it is believed.
- Counter / weakness: doubt; and a never-liar can't be told.
- How society treats it: the Ninety-Nine law; tellers are banished when a telling slips and proves them. Honest dealing is prized; a weigher who can't lie is a treasure.
- Buying, selling, ownership, law: a telling can make a thing someone's property, with deed and memory. Money paid under it stays paid; when it slips, the law makes the teller repay.
- First on-page demonstration: ch1 (the telling), ch1 end (the toll gate).
- How the climax uses it: Nell, untouchable by tellings, is put to a public test that the telling about her can't explain; doubt catches through the crowd and the telling slips, then all the others.

### Rules of the World

_The reader never hears this list. It is for the author, so every scene obeys the same rules._

**Tellings**
1. **A hundred make it so.** A spoken story becomes true when a hundred living people in the parish believe it at the same time. Believing means really believing, not "that's a good story".
2. **Only spoken words carry, and belief is catching.** Reading something silently doesn't count; reading it aloud does. Belief caught from someone who repeats the story counts as much as belief from the teller, so a story told to eighty can reach a hundred overnight through the town's kitchens. Tellers call this letting it catch.
3. **When it takes, the world changes to fit.** Things, buildings, papers and posts change in the moment the hundredth person believes; nobody sees it happen, it is simply so. Every mind that has ever told a lie remembers it as always so, back as far as the story needs.
4. **A telling can't touch a living body.** It can't kill, heal, move or change a person's flesh. It can change what others remember and think of a person, what they own and what work they do. (Why Fairweather can't tell Nell away.)
5. **A telling can't change what has happened, and stops at the boundary stones.** What people did while it held stays done: a penny paid at the toll gate stays paid. It holds only in the parish where it is believed. Anyone who has ever lied takes it on as always so the moment they cross the boundary stones, "like a cold"; so an outsider is no witness (ch2, Gran's Hollin story: only a never-liar coming home could doubt it). (Why Fairweather can't make himself king; why Nell can't fetch a witness from outside.)
6. **Doubt unmakes.** A telling holds while a hundred believe it. Nobody can argue someone out of a memory, but when a person sees something their memory can't explain, they begin to doubt, and doubt is catching like belief. When fewer than a hundred believe, the telling slips: the world goes back, and everyone remembers both versions and knows they were told.
7. **Thin before it slips.** When a crowd stands round something a telling made and doubts it, the thing goes thin in front of them, like a reflection in a shop window; the more doubters, the thinner. It only slips altogether when belief across the whole parish falls under a hundred. A fresh telling of the story to the doubters firms it up again.
8. **A never-liar can't be told.** A mind that has never told a lie is untouched: babies, animals, and Nell. They see the changed world but remember the old one. A telling about them changes what others think of them, never their own minds.
9. **One lie ends it.** The first lie a never-liar tells makes her like everyone else. Every telling in force takes her mind at once, and she remembers it as always so.
10. **What a lie is.** Saying what you believe is false, meaning to be believed. Mistakes, jokes and stories told as stories are not lies. Saying the old truth after a telling is not a lie for Nell, because she believes it. Saying "I made the free bridge up" would be.

**Pennyford: law, money and the Book**
11. **The Ninety-Nine.** By the town charter nobody but the sworn Crier may tell news to more than ninety-nine people at once. The Crier is sworn by the Reeve at every spring Moot, hand on the Book, and swears to cry only what two witnesses have sworn to be true (ch2). Telling 2 made everyone remember Fairweather sworn (ch3); after the slip the Reeve remembers she never swore him, so he spoke to the Moot as a false Crier (ch8). Breaking the Ninety-Nine costs a fine (the Reeve sets it). A false telling to more than ninety-nine, once proved, means banishment from the parish.
12. **A telling covers its own tracks.** Once it takes, nobody remembers it was ever false, so there is no crime to remember. Fairweather never speaks to more than ninety-nine and lets the story catch, so on its face he breaks no law.
13. **The Book is paper.** The parish Book (deeds, tolls, posts, rents) changes with a telling like any paper. No record can prove a telling. Only memory can, and only a never-liar's memory survives one.
14. **A slip proves the telling.** After a slip everyone remembers both versions and who told them, which is the proof the law needs: the Reeve can banish the teller and order him to repay what was paid to him while it held (rule 5).
15. **What a telling gives, the law enforces while it holds.** If a telling makes the bridge his, the toll is owed in law; if it makes the cottage his, the rent is owed and the Reeve will see it paid.
16. **The Moot.** Twice a year the whole parish (about three hundred) meets in the market square and the Crier speaks from the market cross. It is the one time a single voice can reach far more than a hundred at once: legal for the Crier, a crime for anyone else.

**Stress test (answered)**
- *Why doesn't Fairweather tell Nell away, or dead?* Rule 4. *Tell that she believes him?* Rule 8.
- *Why doesn't Nell tell her own story?* She is the worst storyteller in Pennyford; she tries at the market (ch3) and at the bridge (ch4, it nearly works). After the telling about her (ch5) nobody believes a word she says.
- *Why doesn't the Reeve arrest him?* He hasn't broken the Ninety-Nine (rule 12), and after ch3 he is the Crier.
- *Why can't the Book or a witness prove it?* Rule 13; animals and babies can't swear (ch2).
- *Why doesn't Nell just lie to save Gran's cottage?* She'd forget everything (rule 9), and the town would have no one left who remembers. She nearly does (ch6).
- *Why doesn't he tell he's king?* Rule 5; and he wants Pennyford, the town that forgot him.
- *Why does the test at the Moot work?* The crowd's memory says Nell lies easily; three hundred people watch her fail to (rule 6). He tells it again on the spot (rule 7), but a telling can't change her (rule 8), and the gap gets wider, not smaller. Then Nell points at a memory the telling left standing: the whole Moot made her Weigher two years ago because she "can't fib about a quarter-ounce" (ch1, ch7). Nobody can explain why they'd give the weights to the biggest liar, and doubt catches. Biscuit's bark is only the last push.
- *Why does Fairweather risk the Moot instead of more alehouse tellings?* The founding story is too big to catch in alehouses; it needs the whole parish at once, from the Crier (ch6).
- *Why not tell that Nell has been banished?* She would stand at the boundary stones telling every carter; he would rather she lied and became one of them (ch6).
- *The toll keeper?* A real man, Fairweather's servant who came with him on the carrier's cart (ch1–2); a telling gave him a job, not a body (rule 4). He leaves up the Hollin road (ch8).
- *Why do all the tellings fall after the one about Nell?* Once it slips, everyone remembers both versions and that Fairweather told them (rule 6). Knowing they were told once, they doubt every story he told, and each falls under a hundred.
- *The tolls?* Paid and stays paid (rule 5); repaid by order of the Reeve (rule 14).
- *Nell broke the Ninety-Nine.* True, and fined (rule 11); her telling was true, so it isn't banishment.
