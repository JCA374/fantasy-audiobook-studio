# Chapter Seven: The Moot

On the morning of the Autumn Moot, Nell Pardy stood on the steps of the weigh-house in Pennyford market square and counted the parish.

It took her a long time, because they kept moving. They came in from the farms in carts, and from Ladle Lane and Shambles Row on foot. They came over the bridge in a long line, each of them putting a penny in the box, each of them making the gate creak. The square filled up from the edges in. It smelled of wet straw and apples and too many people in their good coats. Nell counted three hundred and four, and then three hundred and nine, and then she gave up, because the Hobday boys would not stand still.

Gran stood beside her, in her church hat, holding Nell's hand very tightly. Mrs Thorne stood at the foot of the market cross with the Book under her arm. Mr Hobday, the new Weigher, stood in the weigh-house doorway behind them with the box of brass weights at his feet. The Weigher always brought the town's weights to the Moot, to be counted. And on the steps of the market cross, in the Crier's blue coat, with the brass bell in his hand, stood Lucan Fairweather.

He rang the bell. Three strokes, a pause, three strokes. The square went quiet.

"Oyez," he called, in the voice like warm honey. "Oyez, oyez. Friends, neighbours, people of Pennyford." He let the silence stretch. "I promised you a story. The story of this town, and who made it, and who it belongs to. Every stone." He smiled down at them, and three hundred faces smiled back. "Three hundred years ago, there was nothing here but a ford and a few poor huts, and a man called Abel Fairweather came over the hills with—"

"That's not true," said Nell.

She said it too loudly. She said it as she said everything, like plates falling off a shelf. It went across the square like a dropped tray.

Three hundred faces turned.

Nell felt the heat start in her neck. She felt it climb into her ears and her cheeks. Gran's hand squeezed hers, hard. She kept going.

"Pennyford wasn't founded by the Fairweathers," she said. "I don't know who founded it. Nobody does. That's the truth. The bridge was free until three weeks ago. Amos Bell was the Crier until this month. My gran owned her house until two weeks ago. That man came to the Michaelmas Fair and told you stories in the Half Moon, and you believed them, and they came true. And he's about to do it again, to all of you at once."

For a moment, nobody said anything at all.

Then somebody at the back called out, "Sit down, Nell Pardy!" And somebody else called, "Biggest liar in Pennyford!" And then a lot of people were laughing and calling out at once, and it wasn't the old laughter, the fond kind. It was hard and uneasy, the laugh people give someone who has walked into church in the wrong clothes.

"Nell Pardy." Mrs Thorne had stepped forward, and her voice cut through the noise. "You are speaking to the whole parish, and you are not the Crier. That's the Ninety-Nine. Come down from those steps."

"If she's the biggest liar in Pennyford," said Gran, "then let her lie for us."

She did not say it loudly. But she was standing on the weigh-house steps beside Nell, and she had sold pies across that square for fifty years. There was something in her voice that made the people nearest stop laughing to listen.

"Go on," said Gran. "You all know her. You all remember her lying since she could walk. So let her lie. Shouldn't be hard. Ask her for one, and let's all hear it."

There was a murmur. People liked that. It sounded like a game at a fair.

On the market cross, Lucan Fairweather had gone very still. Nell saw him think. She saw him work it out, quickly, as he did everything, and then she saw him smile, because he thought it was the best thing that could possibly have happened.

Nell knew what he was thinking, because she and Gran had thought it first, at two o'clock that morning, at the kitchen table. He thought he couldn't lose. If she told a lie now, in front of everyone, she would be like everyone else the moment it left her mouth. She would forget the free bridge, and Amos's bell, and Gran's grandfather's name over the back door. She would look up at Lucan Fairweather and remember a Fairweather crying the news since she was a girl, and she would never know. And if she refused to try, she would look exactly like what they all remembered: a liar too sly to be caught.

Gran's plan was the only other way through, and it was the thing Nell was worst at in the world, and best at. She had to try. She had to really try, in front of all of them, and fail.

"What a good idea, Mrs Pardy," he said. "Yes. Lie for us, Miss Pardy. Just once. For old times' sake." He spread his hands. "Tell them I'm an honest man."

The square went quiet again. Three hundred people waited.

Nell looked at him. She thought about the mirror, and the third try, and how close it had been. She thought: I could. I nearly did. And then she let go of Gran's hand, because she wanted them to see, and she took a breath, and she really tried.

"Lucan Fairweather," she said, "is an—"

The heat came up into her face so fast it made her eyes water. Her chest jumped. She hiccupped, so loudly that people at the back of the square heard it.

"He's an hon—" she said, and hiccupped again, and then it came out of her all at once, as it always did. "He isn't. He isn't an honest man. He isn't even Lucan Fairweather. He's Luke Sallow. He's Ruth Sallow's boy from the tan-yard. You laughed at him in the playground, and he ran away when he was twelve, and he's come back, and he's a thief."

The silence after that was a different kind of silence.

Somebody laughed, near the front, and stopped, as if they'd been surprised by it. Nell saw Mr Tuttle, in the middle of the crowd, with Biscuit sitting on his feet. Mr Tuttle's mouth had fallen open.

"Well, that's Nell," said Mr Tuttle slowly, to nobody in particular. "That's just like Nell, that is."

"Friends." Lucan Fairweather's voice came down from the cross, warm and sad and quick. "Friends, you've seen her do this a hundred times. It's a trick. She's practised it since she was a child. The blushing, the poor little hiccup. Who's she ever fooled with it but the kind-hearted? She went red and hiccupped at Martha Gammidge's cheese, and then she told the whole fair it was sweaty. Don't you remember?"

Nell saw them remember. She saw faces go soft, here and there, and eyes turn back to him. He was good. He was so good. The whole square leaned towards the cross, as it always did when he talked.

"Then she can do it again," said Gran. "Can't she? If it's a trick." She raised her voice. "Margery Thorne. You've known her since she was small enough to sit on a scale. You ask her one."

Mrs Thorne was looking at Nell. She was standing very straight, as she did when her thoughts needed room. Then she said, clearly, so the square could hear, "Nell. Tell me my lemon cake is nice."

Nell's face went hot all over again.

"It's dry," she said miserably. She hiccupped. "I'm sorry, Mrs Thorne. It's always dry. You put too much flour in."

A man near the horse trough laughed out loud. So did a woman by the cheese stall. But this was the old laughing. Nell heard the difference. It was the fond kind, the poor-Nell kind, and as soon as people heard themselves doing it, they stopped and looked at each other, puzzled. As if they had found an old coat in the cupboard and couldn't remember buying it.

"Nell Pardy," called Lucan Fairweather, fast now, every word warm and every word certain, "has been able to lie as easily as breathing since the day she was born. Everybody here knows it. Everybody here has seen it."

He was telling it. Nell could hear that he was telling it, right there, to three hundred people at once. She felt it go past her like a draught under a door. She saw faces nod.

And then she saw them look at her, and wait for her to lie as easily as breathing. She couldn't. She just stood there, red to the ears, with her hands shaking, and hiccupped.

Lucan Fairweather could tell them anything he liked about her. He couldn't change her. Bodies were out of bounds. He had said so himself.

And he had given her something, though he didn't know it. He had told them she had lied since the day she was born, and he had told them nothing else. His story had no room in it for the spring Moot two years ago, when the whole town put its hands up for her. A story with a hole in it doesn't fit. Nell only had to show them where the hole was.

"Mr Hobday," Nell said. Her voice shook, but it carried. "Two years ago, at the spring Moot, who made me Weigher?"

Mr Hobday, in the doorway behind her, frowned. "We did, Nell. The whole Moot. Old Mr Crane put your name up."

"And why did he put my name up?"

"Because you—" Mr Hobday stopped. Nell watched him reach for it. Everybody in the square had heard old Mr Crane say it, at that Moot, with his hand on Nell's shoulder: because she can't fib about a quarter-ounce. "Because you couldn't," said Mr Hobday slowly, and didn't finish.

"You gave the town's weights to the biggest liar in Pennyford," said Nell. "All of you. You put your hands up. Does anybody remember why you'd do a thing like that?"

Nobody remembered why. She watched them try.

Then she started counting.

She counted as she counted weights, carefully, as she'd counted eighty-three people in the Half Moon. Mrs Gammidge was chewing the inside of her cheek. The curate had taken his hat off and was turning it round and round. Mrs Lusk was holding her girls by the shoulders and staring at Nell as if she had never seen her before. Fifty. Mr Hobday had picked up the box of brass weights and was holding it against his chest, frowning down into it. Eighty. A hundred. The Hobday boys had stopped fidgeting. Mr Tuttle had his cap off. A hundred and fifty. It was going through the crowd the way his stories went through the Half Moon. People turned and muttered to each other, and the people next to them frowned, and turned, and muttered. It was catching.

Two hundred. Two hundred and twenty.

On the market cross, Lucan Fairweather opened his mouth to speak again. For the first time since Nell had known him, nobody leaned in.

Biscuit had wriggled free of Mr Tuttle and come through the legs of the crowd to the weigh-house steps. He sat down at Nell's feet and barked once, sharply, at the cross. Somewhere near the cheese stall a man laughed and said, "Even the dog knows," and the laugh went round the square. It was the fond kind.

And every person in the square remembered two things at once.
