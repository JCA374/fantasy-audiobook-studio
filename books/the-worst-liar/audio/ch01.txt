Chapter One. Short Weight.

[pause 1.0]

On the morning of the Michaelmas Fair, Nell Pardy stood in the weigh-house on Pennyford market square and tried, as she had tried all her life, to tell a lie.

Mrs Gammidge had put a wheel of cheese on the scale. Nell slid the brass weights along until the beam settled with a soft clank, and wrote down four pounds and three ounces. That part was easy. Then Mrs Gammidge leaned over the counter, smelling of whey and lavender, and said, "Best cheese at the fair, wouldn't you say, Nell?"

Nell knew what she was meant to say. She had heard people say it all her life. You said yes, and the other person smiled, and nobody was hurt.

She opened her mouth to say yes. Her neck went hot. The heat climbed into her cheeks and her ears, and something jumped in her chest like a frog in a bucket.

"It's," she said, and hiccupped. "It's sweaty."

There was a pause. Then Mr Tuttle the baker, who was waiting with a sack of flour, laughed so hard he had to hold on to the doorframe.

"Worst liar in Pennyford," he said. "Always was. Don't take it to heart, Martha. She told the vicar his sermon was long when she was six. In church."

"Well, it was a long sermon," Nell said. She hiccupped again and wished she had kept her mouth shut. She usually did. It was safer.

Mrs Gammidge sniffed, took her cheese and went, and the queue moved up.

Nell had been the Market Weigher of Pennyford for two years. She was nineteen, tall, with big hands that were good with small weights, and freckles that vanished when she blushed because everything around them turned the same colour. The old Weigher, Mr Crane, had put her name up at the spring Moot himself. He had told the whole square it was because she couldn't fib about a quarter-ounce, and he had meant it kindly.

She liked the work. The weigh-house was a stone hut with one wide window onto the square, and on fair day the square smelled of wet straw and apples. People brought her cheese and wool and flour and sides of bacon, and she weighed them and wrote the weights down, and nobody had to take her word for anything. The beam said it. When the beam settled with that soft brass clank, that was true weight, and there was nothing to argue about.

There were sixteen brass weights in the box, from the great pound down to the quarter-ounce. When Nell was nervous, she counted them. She counted a good deal on fair days.

Under the bench outside, Biscuit lay with his chin on his paws. He was Mr Tuttle's dog, brown and the shape of a small barrel, and he believed that the weigh-house was where crusts came from. He was right about this. Nell's grandmother saw to it.

Gran's pie stall stood across the square, under a striped awning, and by eleven o'clock Gran had sold forty onion pies and told six lies. Nell had counted. She had told Mrs Lusk that the pastry was her own mother's recipe, which it wasn't. She had told the curate she was sixty-one, which she wasn't, by thirteen years. She had told a small boy that the pies made you grow, and a farmer that she'd never seen him look so well. None of them hurt anybody, and everybody went away happier, and Nell had never understood how it was done.

At noon, a man in a green coat with silver buttons stopped at the pie stall and bought the last six.

Nell noticed him because the square went quieter around him. It was not that he was loud. It was the opposite. He spoke softly, so people leaned in to hear, and once they had leaned in, they stayed. He had a voice like warm honey and a face that was pleasant without being handsome, and he asked Gran her name and then used it, twice, as if he'd been saving it up.

"Mrs Pardy," he said, "these are the best pies I've eaten since I left home. And I've eaten pies in four cities."

Gran went pink and told him he was a flatterer, and he said he was nothing of the kind. Then he looked across the square, straight into the weigh-house window, and touched the brim of his hat to Nell. She looked down at her ledger at once.

"Lucan Fairweather," said Mr Tuttle, who had come back for a crust and the gossip. "Came in on the carrier's cart this morning, with a big sleepy fellow in a red scarf to carry his bags. Bought a round at the Half Moon before ten. Very free with his money."

Nell asked where he was from. "Here and there," said Mr Tuttle, which meant he didn't know, which meant nobody did.

Nell didn't like him. She could not have said why. She couldn't put it on a scale. He had done nothing but buy pies and be polite, and that was not a crime, and so she put the thought away and weighed a crate of plums.

The fair went on. There was a fiddler, and a man who ate fire, and the ox roast, and the wrestling, which Mr Tuttle lost. The light went gold, then grey. Nell locked the weights in their box, all sixteen, and locked the weigh-house, and went to help Gran pack up.

"Take the last tray to the Half Moon, pet," said Gran. "Mr Hobday ordered two dozen for the evening, and my knees have done a day's work."

[pause 1.5]

The Half Moon stood at the bottom of the square, near the bridge. When Nell pushed the door open with her hip, it smelled of beer and pipe smoke, and it was fuller than she had ever seen it.

Mr Hobday, the landlord, was standing just inside with a slate, counting heads. As she came in, he made a mark.

"Ninety-eight," he said. Then a farmer squeezed in behind her. "Ninety-nine. That's the lot. Sorry, Jem, you'll have to wait outside."

"Quite right, Mr Hobday." The voice came from the far end of the room, from the man in the green coat, who was standing by the fire. "We keep the law in here. Ninety-nine, and not one more." He winked at the room, and the room laughed, warmly, as if it had been let in on something.

Nell knew the law, of course. Everyone in Pennyford did. It was in the town charter, painted on a board in the church porch. Nobody but the sworn Crier could tell news to more than ninety-nine people at once. Gran said the law was older than anyone, and that it had been made because of tellings, the way stories turned true in the cities if enough people believed them. Nobody in Pennyford had ever seen a telling. It was the kind of thing that happened somewhere else, like plague, or opera.

She put the pies on the counter. Then, because the room was so full and so quiet, she counted it.

Some of the ninety-nine were in the yard, smoking, or in the back with the barrels. In the room itself, facing the fire, there were eighty-three. She counted twice to be sure.

Lucan Fairweather waited until every face was turned to him. Then he began to talk.

He talked about the bridge. Nell knew the bridge. She had walked over it that morning, and every morning of her life: five stone arches over the River Penny, worn smooth on top by a few hundred years of carts and feet. It was free. It had always been free. That was why the town was called Pennyford: you didn't have to pay a penny to cross the Penny. It was one of Gran's favourite jokes.

But Lucan Fairweather told it differently.

He told them about the great flood, two hundred years ago, when the old ford drowned a cart, a horse and two children. He told them about his great-great-grandfather, Josiah Fairweather. Josiah had sold everything he owned to build a bridge, so that no mother in Pennyford would ever again stand at the water's edge and watch. He told them how Josiah had asked for nothing back but a penny a crossing, to keep the stones in good repair. And he told them how, in the years since, the Fairweathers had grown rich in other places, and gone away, and let the town forget the toll out of kindness.

"But a promise is a promise, friends," he said softly. "And a bridge is a bridge. And the stones are old now, and I have come home to look after them."

He did not raise his voice once. He said Mr Hobday's name, and Jem's, and Mrs Lusk's, and he put the drowned children in the room so close that Mrs Lusk wiped her eyes. When he talked about the penny, it sounded like the most natural thing in the world. Nell, standing by the counter with the empty pie tray, watched eighty-three people nod.

It isn't true, she thought. The bridge is free. I walked over it this morning.

She looked around for someone else who knew it. Mr Hobday was nodding. The farmer by the door had his hat off, as if in church. Even Mr Tuttle, who had wandered in for a pint after the wrestling, was blowing his nose.

"Tell it at home, friends," said Lucan Fairweather. "Tell your families. Let it catch."

The room clapped. Somebody bought him a drink. Nell picked up her tray and left by the side door, and nobody noticed her go.

It was full dark by then, and cold. She walked home the long way, over the bridge, because she wanted to.

The stone parapet was rough and damp under her hand. The river talked to itself below, the way it always did, and the bridge was empty from end to end. There was no gate. There was no toll house. There was the lamp on the far bank, and the black shapes of the willows, and that was all. She walked across without paying anybody anything, and felt foolish for checking.

Gran was asleep when she got in. Nell lay awake for a while, listening to the house tick, and counting the weights in her head: the pound, the half, the quarter, down to the quarter-ounce. Sixteen. Then she slept.

She was up early on Sunday, as she always was, and set off for church by the river path so as to cross the bridge again. The mist was lifting off the water. The stones were wet.

At the near end of the bridge, on her own side of the river, where last night there had been nothing but the lamp, there was a toll house.

It was a small stone hut with a slate roof and a chimney, and in front of it a wooden gate across the road, with a box on a post. A man Nell had never seen sat on a stool by the gate, eating an apple. As she stood there, Mr Tuttle came up the lane behind her in his Sunday coat, put a penny in the box without looking at it, and pushed through the gate. The gate creaked.

"Morning, Nell," said Mr Tuttle. "Cold one."

Behind him, in the lane, Biscuit had stopped dead. He stood with his legs braced and his ears flat, and he was barking at the gate as if it had come out of the river in the night.
