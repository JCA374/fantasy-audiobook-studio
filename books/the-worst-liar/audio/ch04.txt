Chapter Four. Thin as Glass.

[pause 1.0]

For the second time in three days, Mrs Thorne listened to Nell Pardy at her dining table, with the Book open at the same page. This time she did not reach for her spectacles.

"Amos Bell," she said, "has been a cobbler since he was fourteen."

"Yes," said Nell. "That's what he says, too. And he hums the Crier's call while he works, and he holds his hammer like a bell."

Mrs Thorne was quiet. She had a way of sitting very straight when she was thinking, as if her thoughts needed room.

"I don't remember it, Nell," she said at last. "I want to be clear about that. I've tried. I've sat here and tried to remember Amos in a blue coat, and all I can see is a Fairweather on the cross, ringing that bell, the way they have since I was a girl." She folded her hands. "But I know you. I've known you since you were small enough to sit on that scale. You once told me my lemon cake was dry, at my own table, with your mouth full of it. You couldn't tell me a cake was nice when it wasn't if your life depended on it."

"It was dry," said Nell, and went red.

"I know it was." The corner of Mrs Thorne's mouth moved. "So here is where I am. My memory says one thing. You say another. My memory has never been tested. You have been tested every day of your life, and you've failed every single time." She shut the Book. "What do you want me to do?"

Nell had thought about it on the walk up the lane. She had counted it out on her fingers.

"Gran says a telling slips if enough people stop believing it," she said. "Not argue. Stop. If they see something that doesn't fit. And I think I could show them things that don't fit, if they'd stand still long enough to look. Only I can't make people stand still. I'm no good at it. You saw, at the cheese stall."

"I heard," said Mrs Thorne dryly.

"But you can," said Nell. "You're the Reeve. If you call a meeting, people come."

So on Saturday afternoon Mrs Thorne called a meeting of the parish at the bridge, to discuss the upkeep of the stones. She stood at the end of the bridge in her good grey coat, with Mr Hobday beside her holding his slate, and she had him count every soul who came.

"Sixty-one," said Mr Hobday, when the lane had filled up and stopped filling. "Well under, Reeve."

"Thank you, Mr Hobday. Keep counting." Mrs Thorne turned to the crowd. "You all know Nell Pardy," she said. "Our Weigher. She has something to say about the bridge, and I've asked her to say it to you. I'll only say this first, and then I'll hold my tongue. I have never known Nell Pardy tell a lie. Not once in nineteen years. I don't believe she knows how."

Then she stepped aside, and there was nobody between Nell and sixty-one faces but the cold air.

Nell's neck went hot. Her ears went hot. She heard somebody at the back giggle and somebody else hush them. She looked at the toll house, because it was easier than looking at the people, and she began.

She was bad at first. She knew she was. She said "um" four times in her first sentence, and she said the bridge was free twice before she'd said anything else, and she saw people shifting their feet. Then she stopped trying to tell them anything, and just started pointing.

"Look at the roof," she said. "Look at the slates. The church roof's got moss on it, and the Half Moon's, and every roof in Ladle Lane. That roof hasn't got any. Not a speck. It's two hundred years old, he says, and it hasn't got one bit of moss."

A few heads turned.

"Look at the hinges on the gate. They're bright. Nobody's ever oiled them, and they're bright." She swallowed. "And look at the bridge. Look at the stones. There's a path worn down the middle of it, where everyone walks. Two hundred years of feet. And the path goes straight on, through where the gate is, as if the gate weren't there. If people had been stopping at that gate to pay, the wear would turn. It would go round. It doesn't."

There was a murmur now. It wasn't laughter. Mr Tuttle had his cap pushed back and was frowning at the stones.

"Mr Tuttle," said Nell. "You've been buying flour off me for two years. You talk about everything. You talk about your knees and your oven and the price of salt. Have you ever once, in all that time, said a word to me about paying the toll?"

Mr Tuttle opened his mouth. Then he shut it again. "Can't say I have," he said slowly. "Can't say I ever thought about it."

"And your dog barks at it. Every morning. He's never barked at anything else in his life, except the fire-eater at the fair."

Biscuit, who was sitting on Mr Tuttle's feet, chose that moment to look at the gate and growl.

The crowd laughed, and the laughter was different this time. It was uneasy. Nell saw Mrs Gammidge chewing the inside of her cheek. She saw the curate take his hat off and turn it round in his hands. She saw people looking at each other, the way you look at someone when you both hear a noise in the house at night.

And then she saw the gate.

It had gone thin. That was the only word for it. It was still there, wooden and grey across the road, but it looked like a reflection in a shop window, when you can see the shop through it. Through the bars of the gate she could see the bars of the gate, and through those, faintly, the road going on. The pennies in the box on its post had gone pale, like coins at the bottom of a well.

Somebody gasped. Mrs Thorne had gone still. She was staring at the bridge, and her lips were moving.

"I remember," she said quietly, to no one. "I remember walking across. With my father. He had my hand. And we didn't stop." She put her hand to her mouth. "Nell. I remember we didn't stop."

Nell's heart was going so fast she could feel it in her teeth. Sixty-one people, she thought. Sixty-one people and the gate is going. If they go home and tell it. If they let it catch.

"Friends," said a voice behind her. "Friends, what a cold afternoon to be standing about."

Lucan Fairweather came down the lane with his hands in his pockets, unhurried, wearing the Crier's blue coat over his green one. He nodded to Mrs Thorne. He nodded to Mr Hobday, glanced at the slate and smiled. Then he walked straight past Nell and stood on the first stone of the bridge, with his back to the gate. Now every face in the crowd had to look at him to look at it.

"You're quite right to come and look at the old stones," he said. "Quite right. And you're right about the wear, Miss Pardy, very sharp of you. I'll tell you why that is."

And he told them.

He told them about the winter of the great freeze, a hundred years ago, when the gate had been taken down and burned for firewood to save a family on the far bank. He told them that for twenty years after that the toll was paid into a cup on the parapet, and nobody stopped, and the feet wore straight on. He told them the gate was new because his father had it rebuilt, in good oak, and the slates were new because the old ones went in a storm.

He told them about a child who had fallen from the parapet, and how her mother had paid the toll every day for the rest of her life, as thanks to the stones that held her. That mother, he said, had been Mrs Lusk's own great-grandmother.

Mrs Lusk began to cry.

Nell watched it happen. She watched the frowns go out of the faces one by one, like candles being pinched. She watched the curate put his hat back on. She watched Mr Tuttle push his cap down and look at his boots, embarrassed, as if he'd been caught gawping at something private.

When she looked back at the gate, it was solid. The pennies in the box were bright.

"[Where](+2) was I??" said Mrs Thorne. She blinked and looked around her, and then at Nell. Her face was kind and puzzled. "The stones, wasn't it? Well. They seem to be in excellent repair."

[pause 1.5]

Nell did not go home that night. She couldn't. She walked up and down Shambles Row until her feet hurt, and then, at nine o'clock, she found herself standing outside the Half Moon.

The window was fogged with breath. She rubbed a clear patch with her sleeve and looked in.

He was by the fire. Of course he was. The room was full to the walls, and Mr Hobday was at the door with his slate. Every face was turned to the fire, and Lucan Fairweather was speaking softly, so they had to lean in to hear.

She couldn't hear him through the glass. She didn't have to. She saw him lift his hand, as if measuring something small, a quarter-ounce maybe. She saw Mr Tuttle shake his head sadly. She saw Mrs Gammidge purse her lips. And she saw Mrs Lusk, who had cried at the bridge that afternoon, turn and look straight at the window. Straight at Nell.

Then the door opened, and somebody came out for air, and in the moment before it swung shut, the warm honey voice came out into the street.

"I don't say it to be unkind, friends," Lucan Fairweather was saying. "I'm fond of the girl. But someone has to tell the truth about Nell Pardy. She has been the biggest liar in Pennyford since the day she could talk."
