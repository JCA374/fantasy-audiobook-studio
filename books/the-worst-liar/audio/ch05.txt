Chapter Five. The Biggest Liar.

[pause 1.0]

On Sunday morning Nell Pardy went to Tuttle's bakery for the bread, as she did every Sunday, and Mr Tuttle wouldn't look at her.

He served her. He took her money. But he kept his eyes on the loaf while he wrapped it. When she held out her hand for the change, he counted it into her palm coin by coin, out loud, as you would for a stranger you didn't trust. Then he put the bread on the scale on his own counter, weighed it and showed her the weight.

"Just so there's no argument after, love," he said, to the scale.

"I've never argued with you," Nell said. "Not once. I've bought bread here every Sunday for two years."

"That's right," said Mr Tuttle. He still wasn't looking at her. "And then you go round telling folk my loaves are light." He wiped his hands on his apron. "I'm not angry, Nell. I've known you since you were small. It's just how you are. But I'll weigh my own bread, thank you."

Biscuit came out from behind the counter, wagging his whole back end, and shoved his nose into Nell's hand as he always did. Mr Tuttle called him off sharply. Biscuit didn't go. He leaned against Nell's legs and looked up at her. Nell crouched down and put her face in his warm brown neck, because he was the only creature in the shop who hadn't changed.

[pause 1.5]

It was the same at church. When she went to sit in her usual place, Mrs Lusk moved her girls along the pew to the far end, and the space between them stayed empty all through the service. In the porch afterwards, people talked about her in voices just loud enough to hear. She'd been giving short weight for years, apparently. Everybody knew. She'd told the vicar his wife was sweet on the curate. She'd told the Hobday boys their mother had run off with a tinker, when she'd only gone to Hollin for a week to see her sister. She'd told Mrs Gammidge her cheese was sweaty, out of pure spite, at the fair.

"Well, that one's true," said Nell, before she could stop herself. She went red and hiccupped, and somebody laughed.

She walked home with her hands in her coat pockets, clenched.

The odd thing was that nothing about her had changed. She checked, the way you'd check your pockets after a crowd. She had the same big hands, with the same ink stain on the middle finger of the right. She had the same freckles. When she said, out loud, in the empty lane, "I'm a good liar," her neck went hot and the frog jumped in her chest, exactly as it always had. The telling hadn't touched her. Mr Fairweather had said it couldn't: bodies were out of bounds. It had only touched everyone else's memory of her.

Somehow that was worse. If it had changed her, she wouldn't have known.

[pause 1.5]

Gran was at the kitchen table with the teapot, and she looked up when Nell came in, and her face did something Nell had never seen it do. It went soft and sorry, as it did for a neighbour's dead cat.

"Sit down, pet," she said. "I'll pour."

Nell sat. Gran poured. Neither of them said anything for a while. The kettle muttered on the hob.

"People are talking," said Gran at last. "I heard, after church. About the bridge, and the meeting." She sighed. "Oh, Nell. Getting Margery Thorne involved. Making all those people stand in the cold."

"I didn't make anything up, Gran." Nell kept her eyes on her cup.

"I know you don't think you did, pet." Gran reached across the warped table and patted her hand. "You've always told your stories. Ever since you were tiny. You told me once there was a man living in the well, and you were so sure of it I nearly had your grandfather go down on a rope." She smiled, a little wobbly. "I don't mind. I've never minded. I just wish you wouldn't do it to the whole town."

"Gran." Nell's voice cracked. "Gran, I've never told a lie in my life. You said so. Last Sunday, in this kitchen. You said I was a never-liar, and you said a telling couldn't touch me, and you said—"

"There," said Gran gently. "That's one now, isn't it?"

Nell stopped. Her mouth was open, and nothing would come out of it, true or otherwise.

Gran didn't remember. Of course she didn't. Somewhere in Gran's head, last Sunday's talk had been told over. Nell had come home with a wild story about a toll gate, the way she always came home with wild stories. And Gran had been kind about it, as she always was.

"I don't mind," said Gran again, and patted her hand again, and that was the worst thing of all. If Gran had shouted, Nell could have shouted back. She didn't know what to do with being loved in spite of something she had never done.

[pause 1.5]

On Monday morning Mrs Thorne came to the weigh-house before the market opened. She was wearing her good grey coat, and she had Mr Hobday with her, and Mr Hobday was carrying an empty wooden box with a brass lock.

"I'm sorry, Nell," Mrs Thorne said. She did look sorry. That made it harder. "I've overlooked it for years. Your grandmother is a good woman, and I knew your mother, and I kept hoping you'd grow out of it. But after Saturday, I can't. You stood at that bridge and you made sixty-one people doubt their own eyes, and I let you." She held out her hand. "The key, please. And the weights."

Nell gave her the key. Then she opened the box of weights and counted them out into Mr Hobday's box, one at a time, so that nobody could ever say, afterwards, that one had gone missing. The great pound. The half. The quarter. Down and down to the quarter-ounce, which was hardly bigger than a button and was cold in her fingers.

"Sixteen," she said. "All of them. You can count them yourself."

"I'm sure that won't be necessary," said Mrs Thorne.

"Count them," said Nell. Her voice came out harder than she meant it to. Mrs Thorne looked at her in surprise, and then, slowly, counted them. There were sixteen.

Mr Hobday was to be Weigher now. He was a fair man and he couldn't add up, and Nell watched him through the window all morning from the bench outside, where Biscuit lay with his chin on her boot. Twice he forgot to carry the ounces. Nobody complained. Everyone remembered that he had always been fair.

She sat there and thought about it, because there was nothing else to do.

For nineteen years she had kept her mouth shut, because when she opened it the truth fell out, and people laughed. She had always thought that was the worst thing that could happen to a person. Being laughed at for being right. But at least, when they laughed, they had known she was right. That was what the laugh was. Worst liar in Pennyford: poor Nell, who can't even manage a fib about a cheese.

Now she could stand on the market cross and shout the truth until she was hoarse, and it wouldn't matter. It wouldn't even be funny. Her word had been the one thing she had, and he had taken it, in an evening, by the fire, without raising his voice.

[pause 1.5]

The letter came on Monday evening. It was pushed under the door while they were eating, and Gran opened it at the table, holding it at arm's length because she wouldn't wear her spectacles in front of anyone.

It was on good thick paper, written in a beautiful sloping hand. It was addressed to Mrs Hester Pardy, tenant, Number Four Ladle Lane. It came from Mr Lucan Fairweather, owner of the Ladle Lane cottages. It said, very politely, that the quarter's rent of three pounds was now overdue. He would be grateful to receive it by Friday. If not, he would be obliged to ask the Reeve's bailiff to see to the matter.

Gran read it twice. Then she put it down carefully, flat on the table, and smoothed it with both hands.

"I always pay him on quarter day," she said. "Every quarter, regular, since I was widowed. I don't know how I came to miss it." She smoothed the letter again. "I don't know where I'll find three pounds by Friday, pet. Not without your wages."

Nell looked at the letter. Gran's cottage. Gran's grandfather had bought it, with money from the pie stall, and his name was carved over the back door with the year, and Gran had shown it to her a hundred times. Nell had never seen a rent letter in this house in her life. And Mr Hobday had said, at the weigh-house that morning, that Mr Fairweather had been at the Half Moon again on Sunday night, telling stories by the fire.

"Gran," she said. "You own this house. Your grandfather bought it. His name's over the back door."

Gran looked at her with that soft, sorry face, and didn't answer, and Nell understood that Gran thought she was telling stories again, to cheer her up.

She got up and went to the back door and opened it. The lintel was there, the same old oak. The name was gone. Where it had been carved, there was only smooth wood, with the grain running straight across.

Behind her, somebody knocked at the front door. Three slow knocks. A pause. Three more.

"Mrs Pardy," said a voice through the door, a voice like warm honey. "So sorry to disturb your supper. Might I have a word with your granddaughter?"
