Chapter Two. The Toll.

[pause 1.0]

Nell Pardy stood at the end of Pennyford bridge in her Sunday coat and stared at the toll house, and the toll house stayed exactly where it was.

"That wasn't there yesterday," she said.

Mr Tuttle had stopped on the far side of the gate to call his dog. He looked back at her, puzzled, then laughed. "The toll house? Nell, love, it's been there longer than I have. My old dad used to send me down with his penny." He whistled. "Biscuit! Leave it. Come on, you daft thing."

Biscuit would not leave it. He barked at the gate, and at the box on its post, and at the man on the stool. When Mr Tuttle came back and hauled him through by the collar, he went on barking over his shoulder all the way across the bridge.

The man on the stool finished his apple and threw the core in the river. He was broad and sleepy-looking, with a red scarf. The last time Nell had seen him, he had been carrying Mr Fairweather's bags off the carrier's cart.

"Penny, miss," he said.

There were people coming up the lane behind her now, on their way to church. Mrs Lusk and her two girls. The Hobday boys. The curate, with his hat in his hand. Every one of them put a penny in the box as they reached it, without a pause, the way you'd duck under a low beam you'd ducked under all your life. The gate creaked for each of them. Nobody looked at the toll house twice.

Nell could feel them waiting behind her. The heat was starting in her neck.

She could have said it. She could have said, there was no gate here last night, I walked over at nine o'clock and there was nothing but the lamp. It was true. She had never in her life been able to say a thing that wasn't. But she had learned a long time ago that you could keep your mouth shut instead, and that keeping it shut was nearly always easier.

She put her penny in the box. The gate creaked. She went to church and didn't hear a word of the sermon.

[pause 1.5]

Gran's kitchen on Ladle Lane smelled of woodsmoke and Saturday's onions. The table had a warp in it you could roll a pea along, and Gran sat at the high end with her tea, still in her church hat, while Nell told her.

She told it plainly, because that was the only way she could tell anything. The bridge had been free yesterday. It had been free for as long as Nell could remember, and Gran had always made the joke about the name. Last night a man in a green coat had told eighty-three people in the Half Moon a story about a toll. This morning there was a toll house, with a slate roof and a chimney, and a man in a red scarf, and everyone was paying, and everyone thought they always had.

Gran listened to all of it. Then she shook her head, smiling.

"Oh, pet. I've paid that penny since I was a girl. My mother used to give me two on market days, one there and one back, and I'd spend the second on a bun and walk home the long way round by the mill." She patted Nell's hand. "You've had a bad dream, that's all. You were up late."

"I wasn't dreaming, Gran. I counted them." Nell held on to the edge of the table.

"Course you did." Gran sipped her tea. Then her cup stopped halfway down to the saucer, and stayed there.

She was looking at Nell in a way Nell couldn't remember being looked at before. As if Nell were a word in a letter that Gran had read a hundred times and only now understood.

"You've never told a lie," Gran said slowly. "Have you, pet. Not once. Not a little one. Not even to be kind."

Nell felt herself going red, which was ridiculous, because it was true. "I've tried. You know I've tried. It doesn't come out."

Gran put her cup down. "My granny used to say there were people like that. Never-liars. Babies, she said, and dogs and cats and horses, and once in a long while a grown person, and you could tell them because a telling couldn't touch them." She lowered her voice, although there was nobody else in the house. "When a story comes true, pet, everyone who's ever told a lie remembers it the new way. As if it was always so. That's how it works. But a never-liar can't be told. They see the world change, and they remember how it was."

"Then it was a telling. Last night. You do believe me."

"I believe you believe it." Gran frowned at her tea. "That's the trouble, isn't it? I've got a memory of that penny as clear as my own front door. If it was a telling, that's exactly what I'd have. And if it wasn't, that's exactly what I'd have too." She shook her head again. "I can't tell the difference from the inside. That's what makes them so wicked."

Nell sat with that for a while. The kettle muttered on the hob.

"Have you ever seen one?" she asked. "A telling?"

"Once," said Gran. "When I was ten, I went to stay with my aunt in Hollin, over the hills. A pedlar came through and told the whole village that the miller had been giving short measure for years. By the next week the miller's scales were crooked, and everyone remembered being cheated, and the miller paid back every customer out of his own pocket." She smiled a little. "Then his wife's sister came home from service. She was one of those people who couldn't tell a fib to save her life.

My aunt said she was the only never-liar she ever met. Coming over the hill, anyone else would have caught the story at the boundary stones like a cold, but not her. She said it was nonsense, and she went on saying it, loud, at the well and at chapel. People started to wonder. And one morning the scales were straight again, and the whole village remembered both things at once. Being cheated, and not being cheated. And they knew they'd been told."

"And what happened to the pedlar?" Nell asked.

"Long gone. And the miller never got his money back, because he'd paid it, and paying's a thing you did. A telling can change how things are, my aunt said, and how you remember them. But whatever you really do while it holds stays done." Gran reached across and took Nell's hand in both of hers. Her fingers were floury and warm. "Now you listen to me. If you're what my granny said, then you mind you never tell one. Not ever. The first lie you tell, you'll be like the rest of us. Whatever's been told in this town, you'll remember it the new way, and you won't even know you've lost anything."

"I can't tell one," said Nell. "That's the whole trouble."

"Good," said Gran. "Keep it that way." And then, because she was Gran, she got up and said she'd only had the one cup, which wasn't true, and put the kettle on again.

[pause 1.5]

Mrs Thorne, the Reeve, lived in the tall house beside the church. On Sunday afternoons she did the parish accounts at her dining table, with the Book open in front of her and her spectacles on a ribbon.

She let Nell in because Nell was Nell. Everybody in Pennyford knew that the Weigher couldn't flatter you, and Mrs Thorne had once said, at a Moot, that she wished there were three more of her.

She listened with her hands folded. When Nell had finished, she didn't laugh. She turned the Book round and found the page, and ran her finger down it.

"Bridge toll," she read aloud. "One penny the crossing, granted to Josiah Fairweather and his heirs, for the upkeep of the stones, in the year seventeen hundred and twelve." She tapped the line. "There it is, Nell. In my own predecessor's hand."

"It would be, though," said Nell. "Wouldn't it? If it was a telling, it would change the paper too."

Mrs Thorne took off her spectacles and rubbed her eyes. "Yes. It would. That's exactly the problem with tellings, and exactly why we have the law." She put the spectacles back on. "Did he speak to more than ninety-nine?"

"No. Mr Hobday counted. He stopped them at ninety-nine." Nell felt her ears go hot. "He made a joke of it."

"Then he broke no law. Only the Crier may speak to the whole town, and that's because the Crier swears before the Reeve to cry nothing but what two witnesses have sworn to be true. A man may tell a story in an alehouse. If it's a story the town takes up and tells itself, well." Mrs Thorne spread her hands. "If this was a telling, it was a clever one. And if it took, then nobody remembers it was ever false, and there's nothing for me to try him for. You see the difficulty."

Nell saw the difficulty. She had been seeing it all day.

"The law wants evidence," said Mrs Thorne, more gently. "Two witnesses who'll swear the bridge was free, and I'll have him in front of me by Friday. Can you bring me two?"

Nell thought about it honestly, because that was the only way she could think. Gran remembered the penny. Mr Tuttle remembered the penny. The curate, the Hobday boys, Mrs Lusk and her girls. There was nobody.

"There's Biscuit," she said at last. "Mr Tuttle's dog. He barked at the gate this morning. He wouldn't go through it."

Mrs Thorne's mouth twitched at one corner. "A dog can't swear, Nell."

"I know," said Nell miserably. "I didn't say he was a good witness. I said he was the only one."

[pause 1.5]

She went back to the bridge at dusk, because she couldn't think where else to go.

The lamp on the far bank had been lit. The river was loud after the rain. The toll house had smoke coming out of its chimney now, and a light in its little window. It looked so ordinary that for one dreadful moment Nell wondered whether Gran was right, and she'd dreamed the whole thing.

Then she put her hand on the parapet. The stone was rough and damp and cold, the same as every night of her life, and she knew she hadn't.

"You paid with a face like thunder this morning, Miss Pardy."

Lucan Fairweather was leaning on the parapet a few yards along, looking at the water. She hadn't heard him come. He had a way of being somewhere before you noticed he'd arrived.

"I didn't want to pay," said Nell. She hadn't meant to say anything. It came out anyway. The truth always did. "It's not a toll bridge. It never was. You made it one."

He turned his head and looked at her properly. He looked for a long time. His face was still pleasant, but there was something behind it now, alert, like a cat that has heard a mouse in the wall.

"Well," he said softly. "Well, well. Mrs Pardy's granddaughter. The Weigher." He smiled, and it seemed to be a real smile, which was the worst of it. "Do you know, Miss Pardy, I've been telling stories in four cities, and I've never met one of you."

He pushed himself off the parapet and touched his hat.

"I wonder how long you'll keep it up," he said. And he walked off over the bridge, paid his own penny with a flourish, and went away up the lane, whistling a tune Nell knew and couldn't place.

It was halfway home before she placed it. It was the tune Amos Bell, the old Crier, whistled every morning before he rang his bell.
