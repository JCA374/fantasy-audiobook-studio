Chapter Four. The Archive.

[pause 1.0]

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) went back to her desk, as Mr Pell had asked. She processed the rest of Dot's nine o'clocks and all of her own afternoon, and she did not think about the difference he had told her to think about. She thought about it so hard that she stamped a retired vicar as a garden gnome and had to fill in a correction slip.

At home, the clock was waiting on the windowsill where she had left it. The chair sat in its corner beside it. She did not sit down. She stood in the middle of the room with her coat still on and told them both what Mr Pell had said.

"One testimony," she said. "He says it carries the whole file. He won't tell me whose."

The chair creaked, softly.

The clock said nothing. Then, slowly, it began to tick louder. Not faster. Louder, each tick landing like a fingertip tapping on a table, the way a person taps when they are waiting for you to understand something.

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) looked at it.

"You were in the Archive," she said. "Before. Weren't you? That's why you knew the number."

The clock hummed. One long, warm note, and then the ticking settled back to normal.

The Archive was the one part of the Office no intake clerk was allowed to enter. Everyone knew roughly where it was: down, through Records, past a door with no handle on the near side. Everyone also knew that it moved. Not the door, but the inside. The aisles shifted, the shelves rearranged themselves, and files wandered to wherever they were most needed. The archivists could find their way through it the way sailors read the sea. Everyone else got lost and had to be fetched out by porters, embarrassed, sometimes days later.

There was, according to rumour, one hour of the night when the Archive held still. The archivists called it the quiet hour. Nobody else knew when it was.

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) took off her coat. She wrapped the clock in her scarf. Then she put her coat back on.

[pause 1.5]

The Office at night was not dark, exactly. Nothing in the sideways city ever was. But the lamps along the intake hall had been turned down to a dull orange glow. The desks stood in rows like gravestones, and [Mira](/mˈiɹə/)'s footsteps came back to her from the high ceiling twice over. The clock ticked against her chest through the scarf. In the empty building it sounded enormous.

Records was unlocked. Records was always unlocked, because nobody would want to steal from it. She threaded between its cabinets to the back wall, where the door with no handle stood in its frame, and she stopped in front of it with no idea what to do next.

The clock hummed, under its breath.

She put her palm flat against the door. It was cold, the cold of stone in a cellar. She pushed, and nothing happened. She waited, and the clock went on humming, and the cold under her hand began, little by little, to feel less like a wall and more like a held breath. Then the clock's hum dropped to a single low note and stopped. In the silence the door swung inwards, as if it had simply decided that now was the time.

The quiet hour, she thought. It knows.

[pause 1.5]

The Archive smelled of dust and cold stone. It went down and down in long, sloping aisles lit by small green lamps, and on either side the drawers rose up out of sight. Each had a brass tag on the front, worn smooth by the thumbs of archivists. When [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) reached out and touched one, it was cool and slightly greasy, like an old coin.

She had expected a catalogue, a desk, a system. There was nothing but the aisles.

"All right," she whispered to the clock. "One, four, oh, seven. Where?"

She started walking. After a few steps the ticking grew louder and harsher, almost a rattle. She stopped, turned back, tried the left-hand aisle instead. The ticking softened. A few steps more, and the clock began to hum.

It was like the game she had played as a child, the one where somebody hides a thimble and tells you hotter, colder, hotter. Except that the thimble was her mother, and the somebody was a dead archivist in a mahogany case.

She walked for what felt like a long time. Left, right, down a short flight of steps, along a row of drawers so old their tags had worn blank. Twice the ticking turned harsh and she had to go back. Once she passed a section where the clock gave an odd, wavering sound, neither hot nor cold. The drawers there were newer, their tags bright, and most of them were nearly empty. She read a few names. None meant anything to her. Then she saw a brass plate above the row, and understood. Pending.

They were drawers for people still alive. Waiting, while the dead brought in their memories one at a time.

She did not stop. The clock hummed her onwards, warmer and warmer, round one more corner. Then it went quiet against her ribs, and she was standing in front of a drawer with four digits on its tag.

[pause 1.5]

The drawer was tied shut with green ribbon, knotted in a way [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) recognised from the appeals trays. A clerk's knot. Not a lock. Just a promise that nobody would undo it. Above the knot, a small card in green ink: Sealed. H. P.

She looked at it for a long time. Then she undid the knot, carefully, so that she would be able to tie it again. Then she opened the drawer.

It was fuller than she had expected. Her mother's intake record lay on top. Name, date, cause: heart, sudden, on the ward. Then the classification, stamped in the flat Office script. Negligent. Term: indefinite. And underneath it, on a separate slip, handwritten in a small, tired, slanting hand [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) knew better than her own:

I was not there enough. I know that. I would like it weighed against the rest, if it can be. Ruth.

That was the whole appeal. [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) read it three times. Then she made herself put it aside and go on.

The testimonies were held together with a bulldog clip. A ward sister who remembered Ruth as the steadiest pair of hands on nights. A patient who remembered being sung to at four in the morning. A man from the corner shop who remembered a woman buying milk and biscuits at dawn and always saying thank you. Kind things. Small things. [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) turned them over one after another, and with each page her hope rose a little, because there was nothing in them that would put anyone in a chair.

At the bottom of the clip was the principal testimony.

It was thicker than all the others put together. The Office's clerk had headed it, in the usual way, with the name and relation of the witness. Witness: daughter. And underneath it, the witness's name.

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) read her own name, and her clerk's number written in after it, and for a moment she did not understand. Then she understood all at once.

She had never given a statement. You didn't, when you died. You just arrived, and everything you carried about everyone else arrived with you, and the Office filed it where it belonged. This was what she had carried. It had been taken from her on the day she walked into the Office. It had been sitting in this drawer for three years, while she sat on the floor beside the chair every night and told it about her day.

She read it. She made herself read every line.

She wasn't there. That was how most of it began. She wasn't there for the school play. She wasn't there for parents' evening, either year. She wasn't there the night of the thunderstorm. She was asleep when I got home from school. I had to be quiet. She was asleep on my birthday until three. She locked the bedroom door in the mornings so I wouldn't wake her. I was a sheep and she wasn't there. I was a star and she wasn't there.

It went on for pages. It was all in her own voice, the voice in her head, the voice she used when she lay awake. And the terrible thing, the thing that made her put a hand flat on the cold stone shelf to keep herself standing, was that every word of it was true.

She had not lied. She had only counted.

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) stood in the green light of the Archive with her mother's file in her hands and asked herself a question she had never asked across her own desk. Not who her mother had been. Who she had been, to put her mother there. Who she was now, a clerk who had spent three years blaming the Office for a sentence she had written herself.

The clock against her chest had gone utterly silent.

[pause 1.5]

She put the file back in the order she had found it. She tied the green ribbon in the clerk's knot. Her fingers shook, and the knot came out a little loose, and she let it.

The way out seemed shorter. The clock hummed her through the turns without fuss, as if it wanted her gone before the quiet hour ended. But in the Pending section it stopped her once more, with a single note so sharp she almost dropped it.

She looked where it wanted her to look.

A drawer, low down, at the level of her knees. Its tag read MIRA, and nothing else. It was not tied with ribbon but sealed with a disc of dark red wax, stamped with a symbol she had seen only once, in the training manual. Clerk in service. Not to be opened while the clerk serves. So clerks counted as pending too. Dead, and still waiting to be weighed.

The drawer was thicker than she would have guessed. Much thicker than any of the others in that row.

She did not touch it. She stood looking at it while the clock hummed against her ribs, and she thought, clearly: somebody has been remembering me. Then she thought: of course they have. I've been dead three years. Everyone I knew who's died since has brought me in with them. That's all it is.

It didn't feel like all it was.

At the door with no handle, she unwrapped the clock and held it up in both hands, so that its face was level with hers.

"You worked here," she said. "For a long time. Didn't you?"

A hum, low and steady.

"And when you stopped, they processed you. And now you're going to be put on a mantelpiece somewhere, in somebody's sitting room, miles away from all this." She swallowed. "I'll sort it. I don't know how yet. But I'll get you put somewhere you can hear it. The Archive. I promise."

The clock was silent for a moment. Then it hummed. The warmest sound she had ever heard it make.

[pause 1.5]

She got home just before the hour that passed for dawn in the sideways city. The flat was cold, the way it always was. She set the clock on the windowsill, took off her coat, and crossed the room to the chair.

She did not know what she was going to say to it. Sorry, maybe. Or nothing. She sat down on the floor in her usual place and leaned her head back against the cushion, the way she did every single night.

It was cold.

Not cool. Cold, the way the drawers in the Archive had been cold, the way a chair is cold when nobody has sat in it for a long time.

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) lifted her head and stared at it. She put her hand flat on the cushion. Nothing. No warmth at all, for the first time in three years.

The chair did not creak.
