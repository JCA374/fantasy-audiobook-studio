Chapter Three. Witness.

[pause 1.0]

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) was at Dot's desk by half past eight, with Dot's files squared in front of her and her own pen laid beside them, which she straightened three times.

The intake hall was quiet at that hour. Most of the clerks came in on the stroke of nine, the way the dead did, and the long row of chairs stood empty under the lamps. [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) had read the first file twice already. It was thin, as most of them were. A life reduced to a few dozen testimonies. A postman who thought she was always in. A doctor who thought she should have come in sooner. A grandson who remembered a tin of shortbread on a high shelf.

None of them mentioned Ruth.

That was normal. [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) knew it was normal. A file only held what other people had carried in with them when they died, and most of the people who remembered Agnes Hale best were still alive somewhere, carrying her about without knowing it. The Office could not ask the living. It could only ask the dead, and only when they turned up.

At nine o'clock the first stamp in the hall came down, and the chair across [Mira](/mˈiɹə/)'s desk was no longer empty.

She was smaller than [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) remembered. Everyone was, she supposed, from the other side. A little old woman in a brown cardigan with bulging pockets, her white hair pinned flat, her handbag clutched on her knees as if this were a doctor's waiting room and someone might try to take it. She looked round the hall with great interest and no fear at all.

"Well," said Agnes Hale. "This is a lot of desks."

"Good morning, Mrs Hale," [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) said. Her voice came out almost normal. "My name is [Mira](/mˈiɹə/). I'll be handling your intake today."

The old woman turned and looked at her properly for the first time. Her eyes were pale and watery and very sharp.

"You're Ruth's girl," she said.

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/)'s pen slid out of her fingers and rolled across the blotter. She caught it before it fell.

"Yes," she said. "I am."

"Thought so. You've got her chin." Mrs Hale nodded to herself, satisfied, the way she might have confirmed the price of eggs. "You used to sit on the stairs. In your nightie, with your knees up. Thought nobody could see you."

"I didn't know anyone could." [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) felt her face go hot.

"Everyone could, pet. It was a very small house." Mrs Hale patted her handbag. "Is this heaven, then? It's very brown."

[pause 1.5]

There was a procedure for this. [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) had learned it in her first week and used it perhaps twice since. When the newly dead remembered somebody whose case was still open, a clerk could take a supplementary statement. That was the Office's phrase for asking a dead woman what she knew about another dead woman, and writing it down.

It had rules. You could only add. Nothing in a file was ever crossed out, not even a mistake, not even a lie. If a witness got something wrong, the wrong thing stayed in the file for good, and the only remedy was for someone else to come along later and remember it better. The Office believed that the truth about a person was a matter of weight, not of correction. Put enough on the scale, and it would settle where it should.

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) slid the form across the desk and uncapped her pen.

"Mrs Hale, before I process your file, I'd like to ask you about my mother. About Ruth. Anything you remember."

"Oh, I remember Ruth." The old woman settled back. "Tired. That's the first thing. She was always tired, that girl. Grey under the eyes. Always on the stairs, going up or coming down. Never stopped long enough to have a cup of tea."

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) wrote: Always tired. On the stairs. Her handwriting looked steady. She was glad of that.

"Out every night," Mrs Hale went on. "I'd hear the door bang. Half past seven, regular as a clock. Some of the neighbours didn't like it, a woman out all night with a little one upstairs. Talked about it, they did. Not to her face."

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) wrote that down too. It was the same as her own memory. It was exactly what she was afraid of.

"She lent me her coat once," Mrs Hale said. "The winter my pipes froze. Big navy coat, lovely and warm. I never gave it back. She never asked." She frowned. "That's not very much, is it?"

"It's something," [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) said, though she wasn't sure it was.

"I'm sorry, pet. I'm eighty-nine. I was eighty-nine. It's all a bit soft round the edges." Mrs Hale looked down at her own hands, as if she were checking they were still there. They were. For now.

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) thanked her and turned the form over. A coat. A tired woman on the stairs. A door banging at half past seven. It was nothing. It was barely a page. She thought of the clock humming in the dark of her flat and felt stupid for having hoped.

"Oh," said Mrs Hale suddenly. "And the light, of course."

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) looked up.

"The light on the first landing. Your mum asked me to keep it on." The old woman nodded firmly, pleased to have found something solid. "First week she moved in, she knocked on my door. 'Mrs Hale,' she said, 'I'm on nights at the hospital. My girl sits on the stairs till she falls asleep. Would you leave the landing light on, so she's not in the dark?' And she gave me money for the bulbs. Every month, in an envelope, under my door. Never missed once."

The hall seemed to have gone a long way off. [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) could hear stamps coming down at the other desks, but from a great distance, as if through water.

"For how long?" she asked.

"Oh, years. Till you were too big for the stairs. Twelve years, maybe. Thirteen." Mrs Hale smiled at something [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) couldn't see. "I'd look up sometimes, on my way to the lav. There you'd be, fast asleep with your head on the banister. And that light on you like a little moon."

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) wrote it down. All of it. Her hand was not steady anymore, and the letters leaned, but she wrote every word, because that was what you did. You only ever added.

[pause 1.5]

When the statement was signed, [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) opened Mrs Hale's own file and read it properly. She had hardly looked at it before. That was a failure of duty, and she noticed it and decided to feel bad about it later.

It was a kind file. Not thick, but kind. People remembered Agnes Hale as someone who was always home. Someone who took in parcels and kept spare keys and noticed when the milk wasn't collected. Someone who left the light on.

"Says here you're being processed as a lamp," [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) said. "A hall lamp. Brass. A glass shade."

"A lamp!" Mrs Hale looked enormously pleased. "Well, that's all right, isn't it?"

"Most people would take lamp." [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) hesitated, then asked the question, the way she always did. "You want to appeal?"

"Appeal? Whatever for?" The old woman laughed, a short dry sound like paper. "I always liked being the one who left it on."

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) stamped the file. No appeal filed. She watched the edges of Agnes Hale go soft and bright, watched the brown cardigan fade to brass and the white hair to a curve of frosted glass. For a moment, before the shape went altogether, the old woman reached across the desk and patted [Mira](/mˈiɹə/)'s hand.

"She was a good girl, your mum," Mrs Hale said. "Tired. But good."

Then there was a lamp on the chair. A modest brass hall lamp with a glass shade. When [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) lifted it to the shelf, it was warm.

[pause 1.5]

The resubmission form was three pages long. [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) filled it in during her break, with Mrs Hale's statement clipped to the back and her mother's file number written at the top in figures, all four digits, plain as anything. Grounds: new evidence. Supplementary statement taken at intake. She signed it and walked it to the appeals tray herself, which clerks never did, and put it in, and did not let herself look at it again.

Time in the Office moved more like weather than a schedule. Sometimes an answer took four years. Sometimes it came back before the ink was dry.

This one came back in about an hour, in the shape of a porter who stood beside [Mira](/mˈiɹə/)'s desk and said, "Mr Pell would like to see you. Now, if it's convenient. And if it isn't."

[pause 1.5]

Mr Pell's office was at the top of a narrow staircase behind the appeals hall, and the staircase turned three times on the way up. [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) counted the steps without meaning to. Forty. Not seventy-one. It still felt like the same climb.

She had never been inside. The room was small and fiercely tidy, with one window that looked out on grey sky and a desk that held a blotter, an inkwell and a single green fern in a pot. Mr Pell sat behind it. He was tall and thin and grey all over, as if he had been left out in the rain for a few hundred years and had faded evenly. Mrs Hale's statement lay on the blotter in front of him, and on top of it, [Mira](/mˈiɹə/)'s resubmission.

"Please sit down, [Mira](/mˈiɹə/)." He had a soft, precise voice, the voice of a man who had read a great many forms and found most of them disappointing. "I'm afraid this is about your mother's file."

She sat.

"I'm afraid I can't accept it," Mr Pell said. "I want to be clear that I've read it. I've read it twice. It's a good statement. It's a kind statement. I don't doubt a word."

"Then why—"

"Because it is one kind memory," he said gently, "and it has to be weighed against the principal testimony. And I'm afraid it doesn't come close."

"The principal testimony." [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) heard herself repeat it. "What does that mean?"

"It means that one testimony in your mother's file carries most of its weight. Far more than the rest put together. It's what tipped the review, and I'm afraid it's what holds it now. A coat and a landing light won't shift it." He laid one long grey finger on the resubmission. "Nothing short of the principal witness changing their account would shift it. And principal witnesses rarely do."

"Who is it?" [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) leaned forward.

Mr Pell looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked at the fern.

"I'm not able to tell you that."

"It's my mother." Her voice cracked on the word.

"Yes. And you are a clerk of this Office, and the rules say you may not handle the file of your own family. Which you know." He said it without heat. "You also took a nine o'clock intake this morning that was not yours. I noticed that. I have decided not to notice it any further."

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/)'s face burned. For a moment she said nothing. Then she found she could not leave it there.

"She appealed," she said. "My mother. At her own intake. Hardly anyone does that. You must have seen it in the file."

"I have seen it." Mr Pell's voice did not change, but he folded his hands on the blotter, slowly, the way a man does when he is choosing his next words with care. "It is a very short appeal. And a very honest one. I have read a great many appeals, [Mira](/mˈiɹə/). Most of them explain why everybody else was wrong. Your mother's did not."

"What did it say?" [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) asked, too fast.

"That is in the file," he said. "And the file is sealed."

"Then what's the point of her appealing at all? If nothing can move it?"

"I didn't say nothing could move it." He looked at her over the fern. "I said a coat and a landing light could not. That is a different thing. I would ask you to think about the difference, and then I would ask you to stop thinking about it, and go back to your desk."

Mr Pell picked up the statement and the resubmission, squared their edges, and slid them into a drawer of his desk. He closed it softly. [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) thought of all the appeals in all the drawers above her pay grade, and understood that she was looking at one of them.

"Leave it, [Mira](/mˈiɹə/)," he said. "I mean that kindly. I sealed that file myself, and I sealed it for a reason."
