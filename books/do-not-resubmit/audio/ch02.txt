Chapter Two. The Queue.

[pause 1.0]

The notice came early that season.

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) found it in her pigeonhole at the start of her shift, tucked between a memo about stamp-pad ink and a reminder that the porters would be collecting from the intake shelves before noon. It was the same small card it always was, the same flat, indifferent script. Case remains open. Do not resubmit without new evidence. Underneath, in green ink, a set of initials and a title: H. Pell, Senior Clerk of Appeals.

She had received fifteen of these cards. She kept them in a biscuit tin under her bed, which she knew was not healthy, and which she did not intend to stop doing. Every time, she had read the card, put it in her bag, and got on with her day.

This time she stood in the corridor outside the pigeonholes and read it four times, and felt something hot climb up the back of her neck.

New evidence. Fine. She would find some.

She walked to her desk with the card still in her hand. The intake hall was long and high and badly lit, a row of desks under a ceiling nobody had ever seen the top of, each desk with its single chair for the newly dead. It smelled, as always, of paper and cold tea. Down the row, the other clerks were sorting their first files of the day, and somewhere a stamp came down with the soft, final thump that was the Office's only real music.

Behind her desk the shelf still held yesterday's intakes. The teapot. The coat rack. The sugar bowl. And the mantel clock, handsome and dark-cased, which hummed faintly as she sat down, the way it had hummed yesterday whenever something pleased it.

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) looked at the collection list clipped to the end of the shelf. Four items, four lines, the porters' tick boxes waiting. She looked at the card in her hand. She looked back at the list.

Then she took her pen and wrote, beside the clock's line, in her neatest clerk's hand: Held. Clerical delay.

It was not a lie, exactly. She was a clerk, and she was about to delay something.

"One, four, oh, seven," she said under her breath, testing it. Her mother's file number. She had not said it out loud in three years.

Behind her, the clock hummed. Not faintly, this time. A low, sure note, like a finger run round the rim of a glass.

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) turned and looked at it. The clock looked back, in the way that clocks look at anything, which is to say it had a face and nothing else to go on.

"You know that number," she said.

The hum came again, softer, and stopped.

[pause 1.5]

Dot's desk was the next one down, close enough to talk across if you leaned. Dot leaned a great deal. She was a small, round, cheerful woman who had been a school dinner lady in life. She treated the dead much as she must once have treated nine-year-olds: firmly, kindly, and with an endless supply of things to say.

"Umbrella," Dot announced, between intakes, holding up a file. "Lovely woman. Kept everyone dry for sixty years. Didn't ask a single question, just said, 'Oh, that's nice,' and went." She stamped it. "I like the umbrellas. They're never any trouble."

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) made a noise that meant nothing in particular. She had the card under her blotter, where she could feel it.

"You're quiet," said Dot. "Quieter. You're always quiet. But today you're quiet like a kettle's quiet."

"I'm fine." [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) didn't look up.

"That's what the kettle says." Dot sat back. "Did you hear about Records? They've had a passage."

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) looked up despite herself. "Gossip."

"Not gossip. Bernard from Records saw it himself. Man came through on Tuesday, old fellow, tweed jacket. File as thick as a doorstep. They weighed him, and he didn't turn into anything." Dot widened her eyes. "Kept his own shape. Stood up, shook the clerk's hand, and walked out the front door. Bernard says the stamp just said passage granted, plain as anything."

"Bernard from Records also says he was once a lighthouse keeper."

"He was, actually. That's why he's so good with the lamps." Dot wagged her pen. "It happens. Once a decade, maybe. Somebody gets remembered so properly, all the way round, the good and the bad, that there's nothing to turn them into. They just get to be themselves. And off they go."

"Off where?" [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) asked, before she could stop herself.

Dot shrugged, delighted. "Nobody knows. That's the best bit."

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) went back to her file. A retired postman, coming through as a bicycle bell. She had always thought passage was the sort of story the Office told itself to keep its clerks going, like a staffroom rumour about a pay rise. Nobody was remembered all the way round. People were remembered the way she remembered them: by the parts that had hurt.

She processed the bicycle bell. He didn't appeal. Neither did the next one, a quiet woman who became a library stamp, or the one after that, a builder who came through as a spirit level and seemed to find it fair. [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) asked each of them the question, the way she always did. Each of them shook their head.

Somewhere in the middle of the morning she realised she was asking it faster than usual, as if she wanted it over with.

[pause 1.5]

The incoming roster was a long sheet of grey paper that came up from somewhere below the Office every morning and was divided between the desks by a supervisor nobody ever saw. Clerks were not supposed to read anybody's section but their own. It was one of the first rules [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) had been taught, three years ago, by a tired woman who had since become a very nice umbrella stand.

"So nobody goes looking," the woman had said. "For their own."

At lunch, Dot went to the canteen, and her section of the roster lay on her desk, weighted with a mug.

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) did not decide to read it. That was what she told herself afterwards. She simply stood up to stretch, and her shadow fell across Dot's desk, and she happened to look down.

Thirty names, each with a line of address and a time of arrival. Old men, mostly, that day. A child, which she skipped over with the practised flinch of anyone who had worked intake for more than a week. A woman from the coast. A man with three middle names.

And at nine o'clock tomorrow morning: Agnes Hale. Flat One, Twenty-Two Albion Street.

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) read it twice. Then she sat down, carefully, in her own chair, because her legs had made a decision on her behalf.

Albion Street. The tall, narrow house with the black railings, the stairs that turned three times on the way up, the shared front door that never quite shut. Her mother's flat had been at the top. And on the ground floor, behind a door with a brass knocker shaped like a fox, there had been an old woman with a cardigan full of cough sweets. She had been old even when [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) was small. Apparently she had gone on being old for a remarkably long time.

Mrs Hale. Agnes. [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) had never once thought of her as having a first name.

She remembered the stairs better than she remembered the woman. Seventy-one of them, counted so many times that the number still sat in her like a song. She remembered sitting on the top step in her nightie, on the nights her mother worked, listening to the house tick and settle below her. Mum went out at half past seven in her blue uniform and her grey cardigan, and the door clicked, and her footsteps went down and down and round and round until the front door banged. After that it was just [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) and the stairwell and the long hours until morning.

There had always been a light on, down there in the stairwell. A small bulb in a glass shade on the first landing, burning all night. [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) had never wondered about it. She had assumed it belonged to the landlord, the way the railings and the broken letterbox belonged to the landlord, the way most things belonged to someone who was not her mother.

Her mother came home at seven in the morning, smelling of hospital soap. She slept through the afternoons with the curtains shut. She missed the school play, the parents' evenings, the year [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) was a sheep and the year [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) was a star. Those were the things [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) remembered. She had remembered them carefully, for a long time.

She knew Mum, [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) thought. She lived under us for twenty years. She saw her every day on the stairs.

It was new evidence. It was walking towards the Office right now, on whatever road the dead used. At nine o'clock tomorrow morning it would sit down in Dot's chair, and Dot would ask it about Dot's own business, and it would be stamped and shelved and gone by ten.

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) sat without moving for most of her lunch hour. Then she went to find Dot.

[pause 1.5]

Dot was in the canteen, eating a currant bun with the dedication of a woman who no longer needed to eat but saw no reason to stop.

"I need to swap shifts with you," [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) said. "Tomorrow morning. I'll take your nine o'clocks. You take my afternoon."

Dot finished chewing. She looked at [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) for a long moment, and [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) could see her doing the sum. [Mira](/mˈiɹə/), who had never swapped a shift. [Mira](/mˈiɹə/), who had been quiet like a kettle all day. [Mira](/mˈiɹə/), standing by the canteen urn with her hands in fists.

"Your afternoon's got the Hendersons," Dot said. "Whole family. Coach trip."

"I know," said [Mira](/mˈiɹə/).

"Five of them, and they'll all want to know why Grandad got a better object than they did."

"I know. I'm sorry."

Dot brushed a currant off her sleeve. "Two weeks of tea runs," she said. "Milk, two sugars. And you bring me a bun on Fridays."

"Done," said [Mira](/mˈiɹə/), without a pause.

"I'm not asking," Dot said, and she did not look away. "I want you to notice that. I'm not asking you anything."

"I noticed," said [Mira](/mˈiɹə/). "Thank you."

The swap had to go in the shift ledger, which lived on a lectern at the end of the hall, chained to the floor as if somebody might steal it. [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) wrote the change in herself, in the same neat hand she had used for the clock. Nine o'clock intakes, D. to M. She signed it with her name and her clerk's number.

Anybody who ever checked would see it. Anybody who ever wondered why [Mira](/mˈiɹə/), who never swapped, had swapped that particular morning, would only need to look at who had come in at nine.

She blotted the ink and went back to her desk. The clock hummed as she passed.

[pause 1.5]

That evening, [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) walked home with the clock wrapped in her scarf and held against her chest like a cat that might bolt. It was heavier than it looked. It ticked against her ribs all the way through the sideways city, past the shuttered shops and the lamp posts that lit nobody, up the three flights to her flat.

She unwrapped it on the kitchen table. In the light from the window it looked less like an object and more like a guest who had arrived too early and didn't know where to stand.

"You can't stay," she told it. "You're on a list. You'll have to go back on Monday."

The clock ticked politely.

The flat was as cold as ever. She put the kettle on, then took it off again without making anything, and carried the clock across the room to the windowsill. She set it there, beside the chair, where the two of them could see each other, if either of them could see.

Then she sat on the floor, in her usual place, and leaned her head against the cushion. It was warm.

"I found somebody," she said. "Mrs Hale. From downstairs. Do you remember her? The fox on the door." She swallowed. "She's coming in tomorrow. I'm going to ask her about you."

For a long moment, nothing happened.

Then the chair creaked. One long, low note, the way old wood settles when someone shifts their weight in it.

And on the windowsill, the clock answered. A hum, deep and steady, that went on and on until [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) could feel it in the floorboards, in the cushion, in her own back. The two of them were talking over her head, in a language she had never been taught.
