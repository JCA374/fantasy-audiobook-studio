Chapter Six. The Hearing.

[pause 1.0]

The hearing was set for the following morning, in a room [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) had never known the Office possessed.

The porters came for the chair at first light. There were two of them, broad and silent, in brown coats. They knocked on [Mira](/mˈiɹə/)'s door with the particular patience of men who were used to collecting things people did not want to give up.

"Furniture for the hearing," said the first one, consulting a docket. "One chair, wood, cushioned. Is that it, miss?"

"Yes," said [Mira](/mˈiɹə/). "That's her."

She had not slept. She had sat on the floor by the door all night again, with her notes in her lap, and the cushion across the room had stayed cold. Now she watched the porters lift the chair between them, carefully, the way you would lift someone old, and she found she could not stand to watch it go down the stairs without a word.

"Wait," she said.

They waited. [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) crossed the room. She put her hand on the worn arm of the chair, where the wood had been rubbed smooth by somebody else's hands, a long time ago.

"I'm going to tell them," she said. "All of it. I'm sorry it took so long."

The chair did not creak. But on the windowsill, behind her, the clock gave a single soft hum, as if to say that it had heard, even if nobody else had.

The porters carried her mother down the three flights and out into the sideways city, and [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) followed with her coat buttoned wrong and her notes in her pocket.

[pause 1.5]

At the Office, a porter led her to it, down a corridor off the appeals hall that she was fairly sure had not been there the day before. The room at the end was long and windowless, with a high bench along the far wall and three tall chairs behind it. A few rows of benches for the public. A small table to one side, with a stack of files on it. And in the middle of the bare floor, under a single hanging lamp, a dark wooden chair with worn arms and a thin cushion.

They had brought her mother from the flat. [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) had known they would. It still stopped her in the doorway.

"Go on, pet," said the porter, not unkindly. "Witness stands by the furniture."

She walked to the middle of the room and stood beside the chair, where she always stood. Close enough to touch it. She did not touch it. She could feel the cold coming off the cushion even from there.

Mr Pell sat at the small table with the files in front of him and a pair of reading glasses on his nose. He did not look at her. That, she understood, was a courtesy.

Behind her, a door opened and shut, and somebody shuffled into the public benches with a great deal of rustling. [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) glanced round. Dot sat in the front row, in her best cardigan, with a paper bag on her knee.

"Swapped my shift," Dot mouthed. "Two weeks of tea runs. Each."

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) wanted to laugh. She found, to her surprise, that she nearly could.

Then the three weighers came in and took their seats behind the high bench, and the room went still.

[pause 1.5]

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) had never seen a weigher before. Very few clerks had. They wore grey, and they were neither old nor young, and their faces were hard to keep hold of. If she looked straight at one, she saw a perfectly ordinary person. If she looked away for a moment, she could not have said whether it was a man or a woman. The one in the middle had a small brass bell in front of her, and she rang it once.

"File one, four, oh, seven," she said. "Ruth. Classified negligent, indefinite term. Under appeal by the deceased, four years. Personal appeal now filed by the principal witness, a clerk in service. Mr Pell, for the Office."

Mr Pell stood. "Thank you."

"The principal testimony, please."

He opened the top file. He took off his reading glasses, polished them on a grey handkerchief, and put them back on. Then he began to read.

He read it exactly as it was written. That was his duty, and he did his duty. He did not hurry it and he did not soften it. She wasn't there for the school play. She wasn't there for parents' evening, either year. His soft, precise voice carried every word to the back of the room. She was asleep on my birthday until three. She locked the bedroom door in the mornings, so I wouldn't wake her.

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) stood beside the chair and listened to her own memories, read aloud in a stranger's voice, in front of her mother and her friend and three grey judges. Each one landed like a dropped stone. I was a sheep and she wasn't there. I was a star and she wasn't there.

She did not interrupt. She did not shake her head. Every word of it was true, and she had come here to say so.

It took a long time. When Mr Pell finally closed the file, the silence in the room was loud.

"Witness," said the middle weigher. "Do you contest any part of this testimony?"

"No," said [Mira](/mˈiɹə/). "It's all true."

"Then why are you here?" The middle weigher's voice was not unkind.

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) looked down at the chair. Then she looked back up at the bench.

"Because it isn't all of it," she said.

[pause 1.5]

She had thought she would need notes. She had written some in the night, by the door, on the backs of old notices. She left them in her pocket. She did not need them.

"She came home at seven in the morning," [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) said. "After a twelve-hour night on the ward. She'd come up seventy-one stairs, and she'd sit down in the armchair by the window without even taking her coat off. And she'd hold out her arms. And I'd climb into her lap in my school jumper, and we'd sit there for ten minutes, every morning, before I went to school. Every single morning. For years." She swallowed. "I left that out."

The weighers said nothing. The weigher on the left had picked up a pen.

"She left me notes on the kitchen table. Every afternoon, so I'd have something from her when I got home. What to have for tea. How many fish fingers. Love you. Back at seven. I had a shoebox full of them. I left that out too."

"She gave her winter coat to the woman downstairs, the year her pipes froze. She paid that same woman, every month, in an envelope, to leave the landing light on, because I used to sit on the stairs in the dark while she was at work." [Mira](/mˈiɹə/)'s voice wavered, and she let it. "That's in Mrs Hale's statement. Supplementary, taken at intake. I'd like it added to the file, if it isn't already."

"It is now," said Mr Pell quietly, from his table. He did not look up. He slid a page from his own folder across to the weighers' clerk.

"I used to fall asleep at the top of the stairs," [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) said. "Every night she worked. And I used to wake up in my own bed, every morning, tucked in. I never once asked myself how I got there." She stopped. Her eyes were stinging. "I think I know now."

The middle weigher waited. [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) took a breath that hurt.

"And there's one more thing. It's about me, not her, but I think it belongs in the file." She looked straight at the bench. "When I grew up and moved out, I stopped ringing her. Not all at once. Just less, and then hardly at all. She used to leave messages, and I didn't call back, because I was still angry about the play and the sheep and the star. I was still counting. And then she died on the ward, in the middle of a night shift, and I didn't find out until the next afternoon." Her voice cracked on the last word. "I wasn't there either. That should go on the scale with the rest."

The room was utterly silent. In the front row, Dot had both hands pressed over her mouth.

The weigher on the right leaned forward. It was the first time any of them but the middle one had moved.

"Witness," said the weigher on the right. Its voice was dry and level, and [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) could not afterwards have said whether it was a man's or a woman's. "You have been a clerk of this Office for three years. You know as well as anyone that a testimony given out of guilt weighs no truer than one given out of anger. How are we to know that this is not simply guilt?"

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) had expected the question. She had lain awake with it. Still, it took her a moment to answer.

"Because guilt would tell you she was perfect," she said. "She wasn't. She was tired all the time, and she missed the play, and she wasn't there when I wanted her. I'm not taking any of that back. I'm saying she was both. She was gone all night, and she was there every morning. I only ever told you half."

The weigher on the right looked at her a moment longer. Then it sat back and wrote something down.

"All of what I said before is true," [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) said. "All of this is true too. I just didn't count it. I'd like it counted now."

[pause 1.5]

The middle weigher looked at the other two. Something passed between them that [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) could not read. Then she rang the little brass bell, once.

"The file will be weighed," she said.

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) had imagined scales. Brass pans, a beam, a needle. There was nothing of the kind. The weighers simply sat, without moving, with their hands flat on the bench in front of them. The lamp above the chair seemed to grow brighter, and the room seemed to grow smaller around it, as if everything in it were leaning in to look.

And the chair creaked.

It was not the soft, single creak that [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) knew from every evening of the last three years. It was a long, low groan of old wood, the sound of a heavy weight shifting, slowly, from one side to another. It went on and on. It filled the room. [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) felt it in the soles of her feet. In the front row, Dot had stopped breathing, if she still breathed at all.

Then it stopped.

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) looked down. Very slowly, so slowly she thought she had imagined it, she reached out and laid her palm on the cushion.

It was not warm. But it was not cold, either. It felt like a hand that has just been taken out of a coat pocket.

The middle weigher spoke. "The weight has moved," she said. "Considerably."

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/)'s knees nearly went. She caught hold of the arm of the chair.

"However," said the middle weigher, and [Mira](/mˈiɹə/)'s heart dropped through the floor. "This is a personal appeal, filed by a clerk in service. Under the rules of the Office, a personal appeal binds two files together. The appellant's own file must now be opened and weighed in the same sitting. No verdict can be given on file one, four, oh, seven until it has."

She turned to Mr Pell.

"Mr Pell. The clerk's deferral has been suspended. The seal no longer binds. You will open her drawer."

Mr Pell stood up slowly. For the first time that morning he looked at [Mira](/mˈiɹə/), over his reading glasses, for a long moment. Then he took a thick folder from the bottom of his stack. It was sealed with a disc of dark red wax.

He broke the seal with his thumb. The crack of it was tiny in the silent room. He opened the folder and looked at the first page, and then he went quite still.

He turned the page. Then the next. Then he looked up, and there was an expression on his long grey face that [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) had never seen there before, and could not name.

"Before I read this," he said, "I should tell the bench something. The principal testimony in this file isn't [Mira](/mˈiɹə/)'s."

He laid one long finger on the page.

"It's her mother's."
