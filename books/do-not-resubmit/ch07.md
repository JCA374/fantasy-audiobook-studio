# Chapter Seven: Passage

Nobody in the hearing room moved.

Mira stood beside the chair with one hand still on its arm, and stared at Mr Pell, and could not make his words mean anything. Her mother's. The principal testimony in her own drawer was her mother's.

Of course it was. Her mother had died first. Everything Ruth had carried about her daughter had come into the Office with her, four years ago, and gone into a drawer marked *Pending*, to wait. It had waited a year for Mira to die. Then it had waited three more, under a red wax seal, while Mira worked a desk two floors above it.

"Proceed, Mr Pell," said the middle weigher.

Mr Pell straightened his reading glasses. He looked down at the page, and then up at Mira. Then he began to read, in the same soft, precise voice he had used for her own testimony, and did not hurry, and did not soften anything.

*She sits on the stairs every night I'm on.*

The words were in Mr Pell's voice, but the shape of them was her mother's. Mira knew it at once. The short sentences. The plain words. The way Ruth had always written her notes.

*I see her from the bottom flight, when I go. Her knees up, her nightie pulled over them. She thinks I don't know. I stand on the first landing every night and look up until Mrs Hale's light is on her. Then I go to work.*

*The ward is two streets away. At midnight, on my break, I run home. Twenty minutes there and back, if I run. I carry her up to bed, and tuck her in, and run back. The ward sister knows. She covers for me. I've never told Mira. I don't want her to stop sitting there. I think it's the only way she feels like she's waiting for me, instead of being left.*

Mira's hand tightened on the arm of the chair until the knuckles went white.

Mr Pell turned to the next paragraph and read on, in her mother's words.

*I come home at seven. She's in her school jumper by then. I sit in the chair by the window and I don't take my coat off, because if I take my coat off I'll fall asleep. I hold my arms out and she climbs in. Ten minutes. It's the best ten minutes of the day. Of any day.*

*I was at the back of the hall for the sheep. Five minutes, in my uniform. Then my bleep went, and I had to run. She never saw me. I never told her, because what's five minutes. I missed the star. I made the costume, out of kitchen foil and a coat hanger, and then I missed it. I'm sorry about the star. I'll be sorry about the star for as long as there is.*

Mr Pell turned a page. His voice had not changed. But he paused, briefly, before he went on, the way a man pauses when the next line costs him something.

*She stopped ringing, after she moved out. I don't blame her. I wasn't there enough, and she'd counted. She was always a good counter. I left messages anyway. I'll leave them till I can't.*

*She is the best thing I ever did. If anybody weighs me, they should weigh that.*

Mr Pell closed the folder.

"There is more," he said. "A good deal more. Notes, mostly. She kept count too, it seems. Of different things." He took off his glasses. "But that is the substance of it."

---

The middle weigher rang the little brass bell.

"Both files," she said, "will be weighed together."

Mira had thought she was ready for it. She was not. The lamp above the chair brightened, and the room drew in close around it, and this time she felt it in herself. A pull at her edges. A softening. As if the borrowed shape she had worn for three years were thinning out, like a cardigan worn through at the elbows. She looked down at her own hands and saw them blur, just slightly, at the fingertips.

So this is how it goes, she thought. This is what it feels like from the chair.

She did not let go of the arm of the chair. She held on and waited, and thought of seventy-one stairs and a light like a little moon.

The chair creaked. A long, low sound, old wood taking a weight and settling under it. And under her hand the worn arm of the chair grew warm. Not the faint warmth of a cushion in a cold flat. Real warmth, the warmth of a living hand.

Her own fingertips came back into focus. Her edges held.

The weighers sat motionless. Then the middle weigher took two stamps from a drawer in the bench, one in each hand. She seemed to hesitate. It was as though it had been a long time since anyone had used that particular stamp, and she wanted to be sure.

The first stamp came down. *Thump.*

"File one, four, oh, seven," said the middle weigher. "Ruth. Term ended. Passage granted."

The second stamp she held out, handle first, across the bench, to Mr Pell.

"The appellant's file is yours, Mr Pell. Her drawer was yours to break. I think the stamp should be yours too."

Mr Pell stood for a moment without moving. Then he took the stamp and inked it. He brought it down on the open folder in front of him, carefully, like a man who had been waiting a long time to do it and wanted to get it exactly right.

*Thump.*

"Mira," he read. "Clerk in service, deferral ended. Passage granted."

---

Mira did not see it happen. She had shut her eyes, without meaning to, at the second stamp.

When she opened them, the chair was still warm under her hand. Then it was not a chair.

It was a woman, sitting on the bare floor under the lamp with her legs tucked to one side, as if she had always been sitting there. A tired woman of fifty or so, with grey under her eyes and grey in her hair, in a grey cardigan with the sleeves pushed up. Her hands lay on her knees. They were a nurse's hands, short-nailed and red at the knuckles. Mira's own hand was resting on her mother's arm.

Ruth looked up at her daughter. She did not say anything for a long moment. Then she said, in a voice Mira had not heard in four years, "Hello, love."

Mira's legs gave out. She went down onto her knees on the bare floor, and her mother held out her arms, the way she had in the chair by the window, every morning, for years. Mira was far too big to climb into anybody's lap. She put her head in it instead, and felt her mother's hand come to rest on her hair, warm.

"I'm sorry," Mira said into the grey wool. "I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I counted wrong—"

"You counted right," said her mother. "You just stopped early. So did I." Her hand moved on Mira's hair. "I went cold, the other night. I'm sorry for that. I knew you'd read it. I thought you'd hate me, once you had. I didn't want you leaning against me, thinking that."

"I didn't hate you." Mira lifted her head.

"No," said Ruth. "I can see that now."

In the front row of the public benches, Dot was crying loudly into a paper bag.

---

There were forms, of course. There were always forms. A passage had to be logged, and countersigned, and entered in the ledger. Mr Pell did all of it himself, at his small table. Mira and her mother sat side by side on the front bench, holding hands like schoolgirls, while Dot pressed a slightly squashed currant bun on each of them.

"For the road," Dot said. "Wherever it goes." She hugged Mira so hard that Mira's ribs creaked, and then, after a moment's consideration, hugged Ruth as well. "You'll have to tell me. Somehow. If it's nice."

When the last form was signed, Mr Pell came over and held out a small white card. Mira took it. It was her own case number, in the flat, indifferent script, stamped across: *Closed.*

"For your biscuit tin," he said. "And you may like to know that the shift ledger now says Dot took her own nine o'clocks that morning. I corrected it myself. In my neatest hand."

On the bench beside them, Dot made a small, delighted noise into her bun.

Mira stared at him. "How do you know about the biscuit tin?"

"I'm the sort of man who notices knots." He looked, for a moment, almost as if he might smile. "Your clock, by the way. Edwin. He was fetched from your flat this morning, while you were here. He's on the shelf outside the Archive door. You can hear him from Records, if you listen."

"Thank you." Mira's voice wobbled.

"Thank you," said Ruth quietly, beside her. She was looking at Mr Pell with a nurse's eyes, the eyes of a woman who had spent a great many nights watching over people who did not know she was there. "For keeping me near her."

Mr Pell inclined his long grey head. He did not say anything. But on his way out of the hearing room, he stopped at the door. He tied his grey handkerchief in a neat, firm knot and put it in his pocket. Mira thought that she might, after all, have seen him smile.

---

They walked home together through the sideways city, because neither of them could think of anywhere they would rather go first.

The flat was as small and badly heated as ever. Without the chair, the corner by the window looked enormous. Ruth stood in the middle of the sitting room and looked round it, slowly. At the biscuit tin under the bed, its lid half off. At the windowsill where the clock had been. At the floor beside the empty corner, where the boards were worn pale in one particular spot by three years of somebody sitting there.

She didn't say anything about it. She just squeezed Mira's hand.

Mira made tea. She made it the way she always did, strong enough to stand a spoon in, and her mother took one sip and made a face.

"That's far too strong."

"I know." Mira was smiling.

Ruth drank the whole cup anyway. Then she held it out for another.

They sat at the little kitchen table for a long time, with the pot between them, and talked. Not about the Office. Not about the file, or the hearing, or the night shifts, or the star. About nothing in particular. About Mrs Hale's fox-head knocker. About fish fingers, two and not three. About the kitchen-foil costume. Ruth said it had taken her four hours and two rolls of foil. Mira said it had made her look like a baked potato. In the end, they both agreed it had been magnificent.

At some point Mira noticed that the window had gone gold. The sideways city did not really have mornings. But the light was coming through anyway, from somewhere, warm on the table and the teacups and the backs of their hands.

"I think it's time," said Ruth.

Mira did not ask how she knew. She simply knew it too, the way you know it's time to get off a bus.

They washed up the cups. Mira put on her coat and found her mother the spare one from the back of the door. It was navy, and big, and Ruth laughed when she saw it. Then Mira left the key in the door, and the lamp on, for whoever came next. Then the two of them walked down the three flights together, and out of the front door, and into the gold light.

At the end of the street, Mira looked back once. The window of the flat was a small bright square in the grey, with the lamp burning in it like a little moon.

Then she turned round, and took her mother's hand, which was warm, and they went on.
