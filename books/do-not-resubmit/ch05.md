# Chapter Five: The Cold Chair

Mira went to work the next morning because she could not think of anything else to do.

The intake hall looked exactly as it always did. The same long row of desks, the same lamps, the same smell of paper and cold tea. The teapot had gone from her shelf. The porters had been. On Dot's shelf, at the next desk, sat a brass hall lamp with a glass shade, waiting its turn. Mira did not look at it more than she had to.

Her first intake was a retired bus driver who came through as a ticket machine. He was cheerful about it. Mira read his file, told him his classification and reached for the question she had asked every soul who had ever sat across her desk.

"You want to—" she began, and stopped.

The bus driver waited. Mira opened her mouth again, and nothing came out. Her throat had closed round the word like a fist.

"Sorry," she said eventually. "Do you want to appeal?"

He didn't. Hardly anyone ever did. She stamped him and watched him go small and grey and full of little buttons, and logged him, and called the next one.

It happened with every intake that morning. Her voice went thin and strange at the question, as if it came from further away than the desk. By eleven o'clock she had stopped fighting it.

At half past eleven a mug of tea appeared at her elbow. Milk, two sugars. Not how Mira took it. Exactly how Dot did.

"I know it's my tea runs," Dot said from the next desk, not looking up from her own file. "But you look like you need it more. I'm not asking anything."

"I know," said Mira. "Thank you."

She drank the tea. It was far too sweet. It helped anyway.

---

At lunch she climbed the narrow staircase behind the appeals hall, the one that turned three times, forty steps. Nobody had sent for her this time. She knocked, and Mr Pell said, "Come in, Mira," before she had given her name.

He was standing at the window with his hands behind his back. The fern on his desk had been watered. There was a damp ring round the pot. He did not turn round straight away.

"The knot was loose," he said.

Mira closed the door behind her and stood with her back against it.

"I tie that knot the same way every season, when I renew the notice," Mr Pell went on. "This morning it was loose. I don't think anybody else would have noticed. I'm afraid I'm the sort of man who notices knots." He turned round at last. He did not look angry. He looked tired, the way the grey of him always looked tired, but more so. "So now you know."

"You knew," Mira said. "The whole time. It was me. My testimony. You knew, and you sent me those cards every season, and you never said."

"Yes." He did not look away.

"Why?" It came out barely above a whisper.

Mr Pell sat down behind his desk. He gestured to the other chair, and after a moment she took it.

"There's a rule," he said, "that a clerk may not handle the file of her own family. You know that rule. What they don't teach in training is why. It isn't to protect the files. It's to protect the clerks." He laid his long grey hands on the blotter. "When you arrived, Mira, three years ago, you brought your testimony with you, and it landed in your mother's file like a stone in a well. The review had been sitting open for a year by then, on her own appeal. Your testimony tipped it to indefinite overnight. And on the same morning, you were offered a clerkship, because you had a quick mind and very neat handwriting, and the Office is always short."

"And you sealed it."

"I sealed it. Because if you had read that file three years ago, the week you died, you would have done precisely what you did last night. And then you'd have done something about it, and all you'd have had to add was grief. Raw grief, and anger. They weigh a great deal, and they weigh in the wrong direction." He paused. "And I arranged for the chair to be placed in your flat. A placement officer owed me a favour. I thought you should at least have somewhere to sit."

Mira looked down at her hands. They were shaking again. She pressed them flat on her knees.

"I can't withdraw it," she said. "Can I. Nothing's ever crossed out."

"No."

"But I can add to it." Mira looked up.

Mr Pell was silent for a long moment.

"You can," he said. "The witness can always amend her own testimony. But you are not an ordinary witness, Mira. You're a clerk in service, and a clerk who adds to a family file is filing a personal appeal. That is the rule. The moment you file it, your deferral ends. Your own drawer is opened, and you are weighed, in the same sitting as your mother. A personal appeal ties the two files together. Whatever is decided is decided for both of you."

"I saw the drawer." She said it to her knees.

"I thought you might have." He did not ask how. "Then you'll know it's sealed, and that the seal is not mine. Not even I can open it while you serve. I don't know what's in it. Neither do you. Everyone who knew you and died before you did is in there, saying whatever they carried." He leaned forward. "I've been in this Office a very long time. I have watched clerks file personal appeals. Most of them came in, like you, believing they had been unfair to someone they loved. Most of them were right. And some of them came out of that hearing as doormats. As boot scrapers. As a stone in somebody's garden wall."

The room was silent.

"You might set your mother free," Mr Pell said. "And then sit on a shelf for a very long time. I'm not telling you not to do it. I'm telling you what it costs. That's all I've ever been trying to tell you."

Mira stood up. Her legs held, which surprised her. At the door she stopped, with her hand on the frame.

"There's a mantel clock," she said. "From my shelf. It's on the collection list, held for a clerical delay. It's at my flat."

Mr Pell's eyebrows rose, a fraction. It was the most surprised she had ever seen him look.

"It worked in the Archive," Mira said. "Before. I think for a long time. It knew the way."

"Ah." Something moved in his long grey face, and for a moment he looked almost young. "Edwin. Forty years in the Archive, and he never once got lost. I did wonder where he'd been placed." He was quiet a moment. "What would you like done with him?"

"Somewhere he can hear it. The Archive. Not a mantelpiece miles away."

"There is a shelf outside the Archive door," Mr Pell said slowly, "that has been empty for a very long time. I'll see what can be arranged. Whatever happens at your hearing."

---

That night the chair was still cold.

Mira came in, hung up her coat, and stood in the doorway of the sitting room. The clock on the windowsill ticked quietly. The chair sat in its corner, dark wood and worn arms and its thin cushion, and from across the room she could feel the cold of it, like a draught.

She did not go to it. She could not make her legs do it. She sat down on the floor by the door instead, as far away as the room allowed, with her back against the wall and her knees pulled up.

It was worse. It was much worse. For three years she had sat beside that chair every night, and it had been warm, and she had believed that the warmth was her mother forgiving her for something. Now she knew there had never been anything to forgive her for, except the one thing, and her mother had kept the cushion warm through all of it anyway. Night after night. While her daughter's testimony held her in place.

And now she knows I know, Mira thought. And she can't bear me sitting against her, thinking it.

She stayed by the door until the clock had ticked away most of the night. She did not cry. She was too tired for it, and too cold.

---

Some time in the small hours, she got up and made tea, because her hands needed something to do.

She stood at the counter waiting for the kettle, and the smell of the tea came up out of the pot, and with it, out of nowhere, a memory she had not had in years.

Seven o'clock in the morning, in the old flat on Albion Street. The front door banging far below, and footsteps coming up and round and up, slow and heavy. Her mother in the sitting room doorway, still in her coat, with the grey cardigan underneath it. The smell of hospital soap. And her mother sitting down in the old armchair by the window, not even taking her coat off, and holding out her arms.

Mira, small, in her school jumper. Climbing into her lap.

Ten minutes, every morning. Her mother's chin on the top of her head, her mother's arms round her, the cardigan warm and rough against her cheek. Neither of them saying much. Then the school bag, the front door, and her mother falling into bed behind a locked door, and the long quiet afternoon.

Every single morning. For years.

Mira had not counted it. She had counted the locked door.

The kettle boiled, and she ignored it. She stood still in the kitchen, and on purpose, for the first time, she went looking. Not for the parts that had hurt, but for everything else.

Notes on the kitchen table, every afternoon when she came home from school. *Fish fingers in the freezer. Two, not three. Love you. Back at seven.* Dozens of them. Hundreds. A shoebox full, once, though she had no idea now where it had gone.

The navy coat that had hung on the back of the door one whole winter, and then had not, and her mother saying it had gone to someone who needed it more.

The way she had always fallen asleep at the top of the stairs, on the nights her mother worked, with the landing light on her like a little moon. And the way she had always woken up in her own bed, tucked in, the curtains closed. She had never once wondered how she got there.

All of it was true. Every bit of it was as true as the sheep and the star and the locked door. She had simply left it out.

---

She wrote the appeal at the kitchen table, before the hour that passed for dawn, on the back of a resubmission form she had brought home and never meant to use.

There was a box at the top for the clerk's name, and a box beneath it for the appellant. She wrote her own name in both. In the space for grounds, she wrote: *Witness amending her own testimony.* Then she sat for a long time with the pen above the page, and in the end she added only one more line, in her neatest clerk's hand.

*All of what I said is true. It isn't all of it.*

She signed it. She took it to the Office as soon as the doors opened and put it in the appeals tray herself, and walked back to her desk without looking round. She did not feel brave. She felt as though she had stepped off the top stair in the dark.

The answer came back before the first stamp of the day had fallen. A small white card, in the flat, indifferent script, waiting on her blotter when she sat down.

*Personal appeal received. Clerk's deferral suspended. Hearing set.*
