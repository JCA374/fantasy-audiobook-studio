# Chapter One: Intake

The soul on the other side of Mira's desk was, as far as she could tell, extremely confused about being a teapot.

"I don't understand," he said. He was still shaped like a man — they always were, for the first little while, a kind of courtesy the Office extended before the sentence took hold. Early fifties, soft around the middle, wearing the cardigan he'd apparently died in. "I was a dentist. Thirty-one years. I had excellent reviews."

"I'm sure you did," Mira said, not looking up from the file. The file was unhelpful, as files usually were — a thin folder of a life reduced to a few dozen testimonies, each one a fragment of how somebody, somewhere, had carried him in their memory. "Says here you're being processed as a teapot. Ceramic. Good glaze, for what it's worth."

"A *teapot.*"

"Could be worse. Had a man in here last month come through as a doormat. Nobody even remembered his name, just that he let people wipe their feet on him for forty years." She flipped a page. "You, at least, people remember fondly. Warm. Reliable. Always ready to pour something out for somebody else."

He sat with that, visibly trying to decide whether it was an insult.

"That doesn't sound bad," he admitted.

"It's not bad. Most people would take teapot." She finally looked up at him, and allowed herself the smallest flicker of the thing that passed for kindness in this job — not softness, exactly, but attention. "You want to appeal it?"

"Can I?"

"Everyone can. Doesn't mean it goes anywhere." She pulled a fresh form from the drawer, already knowing how this would go. Most appeals died in a drawer somewhere above her pay grade, buried under centuries of other people's unfinished grief. But she asked anyway, every time, because the asking was the only part of the job that still felt like it mattered. "Tell me why teapot's wrong."

The man opened his mouth. He was soon to be porcelain, soon to be something that sat on a shelf and occasionally held something warm for people who loved him. For a moment he looked like he might actually cry, which the newly dead sometimes did, in the last minutes before the shape left them for good.

"I don't know," he said. "Maybe it isn't wrong."

Mira nodded slowly and stamped the file. *No appeal filed.* Case closed, in the quiet, unglamorous way most of them closed — not with a fight, but with someone deciding, in the end, that they could live with how they'd be remembered. Even if living was no longer quite the word.

She watched the shape of him thin at the edges, soften, go small and round and glazed, until there was a teapot sitting in the chair where a dentist had been. She logged it, tagged it for collection, and moved it gently to the shelf behind her desk, where it joined a coat rack, a sugar bowl that had by all accounts been a very sweet man, and a rather handsome mantel clock that hummed faintly when it was pleased.

Seventeen more intakes before her shift ended. None of them appealed. None of them ever did, mostly — people spent so much of their lives arguing about who they were that by the time they died, most of them were almost relieved to be told, finally, definitively, what they'd amounted to.

Mira clocked out at an hour that had no real name in the Office, where time moved more like weather than a schedule, and walked home through a city that existed slightly sideways from the one the living used. Her flat was small and badly heated and smelled faintly of the tea she drank too much of, and in the corner by the window sat a chair.

It was a good chair, as far as chairs went — dark wood, a cushion gone a little thin at the edges, arms worn smooth in the places where someone used to rest their hands. Mira crossed the room, dropped her bag, and sat down on the floor beside it rather than in it, the way she always did.

"Long day," she said.

The chair didn't answer. Chairs didn't, not in words. But it creaked, once, softly, settling its weight the way it did when it recognised her voice — and the cushion, when she rested her head against it, was warm.

It shouldn't have been. There was no fire in the room, no sun through the window at this hour. But it was warm, every single evening, in the exact way her mother's lap used to be warm when Mira was small enough to still fit on it.

"I filed another one today," Mira said quietly, not really to the chair, more to the room, the way people talk to graves. "Teapot. Guy let it go without a fight." She picked at a loose thread on the cushion's seam. "I don't know how they do that. Just... accept it."

The chair creaked again — longer this time, a low settling groan that might have meant anything, or nothing, and Mira had spent three years teaching herself to hear it as *I know, love. I know.*

She didn't ask her mother's file number out loud. She didn't need to. It lived in her chest at all times, the way other people carried birthdays or old phone numbers. Four digits. Sealed, marked under appeal, pending review, for going on four years now. No movement, no hearing date, nothing but a small stamped notice, renewed every season, that said in the Office's flat, indifferent script: *Case remains open. Do not resubmit without new evidence.*

New evidence. As if a life could just produce more of itself, on request, for a daughter determined enough to go looking.

Mira stayed on the floor until the warmth of the cushion had worked its way properly into her, the way it did every night, and did not — not yet — let herself think too hard about where she might find any.
