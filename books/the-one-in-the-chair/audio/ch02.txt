Chapter Two. New Evidence.

[pause 1.0]

The letter was already on [Mira](/mˈiɹə/)'s desk when she came in, and that was the first thing wrong with it.

Nothing arrived on a reaper's desk by itself. Files came up from Intake in grey folders, twenty at a time, each one stamped in the corner with the Office seal and the date, or whatever passed for a date in a place where Tuesdays came and went as they pleased. Memos came in grey envelopes, also stamped. There had once been a period when the biscuits in the break room were stamped, after an incident nobody would talk about.

This was not grey. It was a single folded sheet of cream-coloured paper, the heavy kind people buy for writing things that matter. It had been placed square in the middle of her blotter, neatly, the way you would leave something for someone you didn't want to wake.

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) stood there with her coat half off and looked at it.

Then she smelled it. Lavender, very faint, and under that something close and papery: the smell of a drawer that had not been opened in a long time. A living drawer. Nothing in the Office smelled like that. Nothing in the whole sideways city did. The city smelled of dust and ink and, on bad days, the river.

Her body knew the smell before she did. Something tightened under her ribs, the way you flinch at a voice in a crowd before you have worked out whose it is. The bureau in the front room of the shelter. Third drawer down, the one that stuck. Her mother had kept the good paper in there, and the stamps, and a tin of mints she claimed were for the residents.

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) sat down. She did not pick it up. Three years in this job had taught her that the worst things often arrived looking like presents.

When she did unfold it, her hands were not quite steady. She noticed that, disliked it, and unfolded it anyway.

It was her mother's handwriting. There was no mistaking the long loops, or the way every letter leaned forward as though it were late for something. [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) had seen that hand on shopping lists and birthday cards for the whole of her life, and then not at all for four years. She had to lay her other hand flat on the desk before she could read a word.

The letter began, "My [Mira](/mˈiɹə/)."

It went on, "I don't know if I'll send this. I've started it four times. You'll have heard by now what happened on Thursday night, and people will tell it to you in different ways, so I want you to have it from me.

"I won't put her name here. She's had enough people writing her down.

"She came to the office at two in the morning and said she couldn't breathe. She asked me to open the back door so she could stand outside and get some air. I had the key in my hand. Half the rules in that building say I should have told her no.

"But she'd spent eleven years with a man who locked doors on her, and I would not be one more person locking a door on her. So I opened it. She walked out, and she didn't come back, and you know the rest.

"What happened to her after that is mine to carry. I'm not asking anybody to take it off me. But I keep coming back to this, love. The mistake wasn't trusting her. The mistake was a world where trusting her wasn't safe. I would rather be wrong about that door than be the kind of person who keeps it shut.

"I hope it's you reading this. I hope you're not angry.

"All my love, always. Mum."

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) read it three times. The third time, she made herself read it the way she read a file. Slowly, looking for the hole.

There wasn't one.

For four years her mother's case had been built out of other people's memories. [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) knew every one of them by heart. A trustee, who remembered that [Elin](/ˈɛlɪn/) let a vulnerable woman walk out into the night. A neighbour, who remembered hearing the door. A local paper, which remembered nothing at all except the headline and printed it anyway. Dozens of small, agreeing voices, and every one of them said the same word: negligent. Not one of them had ever asked why.

And here was why, in her mother's own hand.

The Office did not care much for feelings, but it was fond of its rules, and [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) knew the rules better than most people who wrote them. Testimony had to be consistent. One fair account did not outweigh many agreeing ones. But a primary account, in the dead person's own hand, carried a weight that nothing else did.

It was evidence. New evidence. Four years of stamped notices telling her not to come back without some, and here it was, on her blotter, smelling of lavender.

Something rose in her chest that she didn't recognise at first, because it had been so long. It took her a moment to name it. Hope. It felt less like a light coming on and more like a limb waking up after you have slept on it. It hurt.

A trolley rattled to a stop outside her door. It was Hollis, the ledger clerk, a narrow man with ink on his cuffs, who pushed the morning's files up from Intake. He was always early. Today he was early even for him, and he stood in her doorway with his hands on the trolley, talking to nobody in particular.

"Boundary Section had their lights on all night," he said. "All night. Somebody's in trouble."

His eyes went to the cream-coloured paper on her blotter, and then quickly away, to the files.

[Mira](/mˈiɹə/) slid the letter under her blotter without thinking about it. She did it smoothly, the way you put a hand over a card, and then she smiled at him and said, "Isn't somebody always." He held on to the twenty grey folders a moment longer than he needed to before he gave them to her. Then he went off down the corridor, faster than usual, and the trolley rattled all the way to the stairs.

She processed nine intakes before lunch. A bank manager who came through as a revolving door. A woman who had kept eleven cats and was gently, and to her obvious delight, becoming a cardigan.

"Where do cardigans go?" the woman asked, near the end, while she could still ask things.

"Out into the world," said [Mira](/mˈiɹə/). "Somewhere. The porters see to it."

"Somewhere with a cat, then. If anyone's asking."

Nobody ever asked. [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) wrote it on the placement slip anyway, under Preferences, in small letters, because it cost nothing and the woman was watching her do it. [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) stamped them all and logged them and was kind in the small, practical ways the job allowed. All the while she could feel the letter under the blotter, the way you can feel the sun on your back without turning round.

Because the hope had a shadow, and the shadow was getting longer.

Letters did not cross from the living world. It was rare, it was expensive, and it was not allowed, in roughly that order. Somebody had opened that drawer. Somebody had carried this across a line that the Office spent a great deal of effort keeping in place, and then left it on her desk without so much as a note.

Nothing in the Office was free. Kindness always came with a form attached. If nobody had handed her the form yet, it only meant the bill was still on its way.

When the corridor was quiet, [Mira](/mˈiɹə/) took the letter out again and turned it over. She was looking for the one thing every piece of paper in the building carried: the little grey stamp, with its seal and its date. The stamp that said this had come through the proper channels and been seen by the proper eyes.

The corner was blank.

Everything that came into the Office was stamped. Everything. Which meant this letter had not come into the Office at all.

It had come in around it.
