# Chapter Four: The Hearing

The hearing room had no windows. It had never needed them. It was a long, low room the colour of old bone, with a raised bench at one end and three chairs behind it. In the three chairs sat three judges whose faces never quite settled into anything you could remember afterwards.

Mira had tried, once, to describe one of them to a colleague over tea. She had got as far as "he had a nose" and given up.

She sat at the small table in front of the bench, alone, with the appeal file in front of her and her hands folded on top of it so that nobody could see what they wanted to do. Behind her were three rows of benches for the public. The public did not usually come. Today, in the very back row, there was one man.

He was thin and very neat and wore grey gloves, which nobody in the Office wore, and he had a notebook open on his knee. He did not look at Mira. He looked at the bench, and wrote, and looked at the bench again.

"Case four thousand, four hundred and seventy-one," said the judge in the centre. His voice was perfectly flat, like a table. "The matter of Elin, deceased, classified as negligent and rendered as a chair, for an indefinite term, being remembered chiefly as the one in charge, who sat in her chair while it happened. Appeal reopened on new evidence. The clerk will read the evidence into the record."

The clerk was grey. Most of the clerks were, after enough years, but this one had gone all the way: grey hair, grey skin, a grey voice that had read ten thousand pieces of evidence and expected nothing from any of them. He unfolded the cream-coloured letter with two fingers, the way you might pick up something that had been in a puddle, and began to read.

Mira had read it perhaps forty times by then. She had thought she was ready to hear it.

"My Mira. I don't know if I'll send this."

Her mother's words, in the clerk's dead voice, in that dead room. It was like hearing a song you love played on a broken instrument. The tune survived. Somehow that made it worse.

He read the part about the back door, and the key, and the woman who could not breathe. He read, "I would not be one more person locking a door on her." He read, "The mistake wasn't trusting her. The mistake was a world where trusting her wasn't safe." He read it all the way to "Mum," and folded it up again, and sat down.

Nobody spoke for a moment.

Then the judge on the left leaned forward a little. Of the two judges who ever said anything, she was the drier, and the one Mira privately thought of as the least dead.

"Well," she said. "That's not what the file says, is it."

"No, Your Honour," said Mira.

"The file," said the judge on the left, turning pages, "says the deceased let a vulnerable woman walk out into the night. This letter says the deceased opened a door for a woman who asked her to. Those are the same facts. They are not the same story."

"The panel notes," said the centre judge, flatly, "that the letter reframes the incident. Not as neglect of a duty of care, but as respect for the autonomy of the person in that care."

"It's a good letter," said the judge on the left.

Mira felt something rise in her chest and pressed it down again, hard, because she had sat in this room for other people's cases and she knew what a judge sounded like right before the word but.

"However," said the centre judge.

There it was.

"The provenance of the document is unverified. The appellant's own filing states that its origin is unknown." He looked at her, or his face pointed in her direction, which came to the same thing. "The panel appreciates the appellant's candour. The panel also observes that the document carries no Office stamp, which means it did not arrive by any route the Office recognises."

In the back row, the man in grey gloves wrote something down.

"Further," the centre judge went on, "the panel is bound by the principle of consistent testimony. One account, however persuasive, does not outweigh many agreeing ones. A primary account in the deceased's own hand carries unusual weight. It does not carry unlimited weight."

"So the letter's not enough," said Mira. She had not meant to say it out loud.

"The letter is not nothing," said the judge on the left.

The three of them leaned together for a moment, the way judges do, and murmured, and leaned apart.

"The indefinite term in case four thousand, four hundred and seventy-one is suspended," said the centre judge. "Not set aside. Suspended. The deceased will remain as she is, but the term is no longer final. Should the appellant establish where this document came from, and by whose hand it came here, the panel will reconvene and weigh it in full. Until then, this matter is closed."

He brought his hand down on the bench. There was no gavel. There didn't need to be.

Suspended. Mira sat with the word while the room emptied around her. It was more than she had had in four years. It was a door off its latch, not a door open.

She knew what was on the other side of that door, too. She had always known, and had always been careful not to look at it. If the indefinite term was set aside, her mother would have a term like everybody else, and terms ended. At some turning of the season she would be released, to go on wherever the released went. If Mira won, her mother would be free. And the chair in the corner by the window would be just a chair.

And the condition sat in her stomach like a stone. Show where it came from. Show whose hand had brought it.

Whoever's hand it was, it had broken every rule there was to do it.

When she came out into the corridor, the man in grey gloves was standing by the door as if he had been waiting for a bus.

"An interesting letter," he said. His voice was quiet and precise, every word put down carefully, like cutlery. "I'm Crane. Inspector. Boundary Section."

"I know what the Boundary Section is."

"Everybody does," said Crane. "Very few people know what it's for." He tipped his head, almost politely. "Good afternoon."

He walked away down the corridor without hurrying, and she watched him all the way to the stairs.

At home she did not take her coat off. She went straight to the corner by the window and sat down on the floor beside the chair, and put her back against its legs, and stayed there.

"Suspended," she said.

The chair creaked. It was a short creak, rising a little at the end. Three years of listening had taught Mira most of what the chair could say, and this one she had heard a hundred times, usually when she came home late without explaining herself. It meant, well?

So she told it. All of it. The clerk's voice. The judge on the left saying it was a good letter. The man in the back row and his grey gloves. The condition. She talked until the window went dark and the street outside went quiet, or as quiet as a street got in a city that ran slightly sideways.

"So I have to find out who brought it," she said finally. "And whoever brought it broke every rule there is. So the only way I win is by handing the panel the name of whoever broke the rules for me." She laughed, not very well. "You'd have liked that. You always said the forms were written by people who'd never met a human being."

The chair did not creak. But when she leaned her head back against the cushion, it was warm, the way it always was. And then, slowly, it seemed to settle more firmly around her, the arms pressing in a fraction, the old wood holding her the way a hand holds a shoulder when it wants you to stop walking for a second and listen.

Be careful, it meant.

"I know," Mira said. "I know, Mum."

She sat there until the warmth had got all the way into her. Then she got up, and took her coat off at last, and went to find a pen. If she was going to find out who had brought that letter across, she was going to have to start by finding out who could.
