# Writing for the Ear

These books will be heard through a single synthetic narrator. A listener can't re-read a sentence, see italics or glance back at who's speaking. Draft with that in mind, and use this list as the read-aloud editing checklist.

## Dialogue
- **Attribute often.** Use a tag or action beat at least every 2–3 lines in any exchange, and on every line once three or more people talk. Plain "said" is fine; it disappears when heard.
- **Give very short lines an emotion beat.** A one- or two-sentence line like "Can I?" gets a small physical action that shows the feeling: `"Can I?" He sat up a little.` (see speech-flow, case 05).
- **Don't lean on clipped lines.** One- and two-word lines ("Yes." "Why?" "I know.") are rare spice: at most one spoken line in twenty (one per chapter is always allowed). Lines whose every sentence is three words or fewer: at most one in ten (two per chapter allowed). Never two terse lines in a row. Keep the short ones for decisions, confessions, interruptions and punchlines (speech-flow, cases 07 and 10). Fix the rest by giving the character something only they would say here: a reason, a deflection or a detail (`"Yes. I signed for her myself."`), or fold the exchange into narration (`She said yes before he had finished asking.`). Don't pad ("Yes, well, I suppose so"): padding is still empty.
- **Put the tag early** in long speeches: `"Listen," Mira said, "the bridge is …"`, not at the end of a five-sentence line.
- Give characters distinct verbal habits (formality, sentence length, a recurring word). With one narrator voice, these habits are how listeners tell characters apart.

## Sentences
- Prefer sentences under ~30 words. Break up nested clauses and long parenthetical asides; avoid semicolon chains.
- Put the subject and verb early, so the listener knows who did what before the qualifiers arrive.
- Vary rhythm on purpose: short sentences land hard when spoken.
- Watch for accidental rhymes, tongue-twisters ("she seized the sixth sword's sheath") and homophones that confuse when spoken (*reign/rain*, *knight/night*, *rowed/road*).

## Typography that disappears in audio
- **Italics** for thoughts, letters, telepathy or foreign words: make it clear in words instead ("she thought", "the letter read").
- No meaning carried by layout: lists, tables, maps, epigraph formatting, all-caps shouting. Describe it instead.
- Numbers: write them as words in the manuscript ("three hundred soldiers").
- Scene breaks: one per change of time, place or POV. narration-prep turns each into a 1.5-second pause. Open every new scene by re-establishing *who, where, when* within the first two sentences.

## Structure
- **Chapter openings re-orient.** Listeners take breaks between chapters, so the first paragraph should name the POV character and the place.
- **Remind about secondary characters** on reappearance ("Oren, the ferryman's son, …") if they've been absent for more than a chapter.
- **End chapters on a hook.** It works even better in audio, where the next chapter autoplays.
- Avoid flashbacks nested inside flashbacks, and keep tense shifts rare. Both are hard to follow by ear.

## Names (also see fantasy-worldbuilding.md)
- Don't introduce more than two new names in a single paragraph.
- Read new names aloud: if they're awkward to say, they'll be awkward to hear.
